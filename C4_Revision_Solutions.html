Analog to Digital Conversion: Worked Solutions

Revision (3a): Sampling Theory & Reconstruction

📝 Question 1

Problem: For a lowpass signal with a bandwidth of 6000 Hz, what is the minimum sampling frequency for perfect reconstruction of the signal? What is the minimum required sampling frequency if a guard band of 2000 Hz is required? If the reconstruction filter has the frequency response \(H(f) = K\) for \(|f| < 7000\), \(H(f) = K - K\frac{|f|-7000}{3000}\) for \(7000 < |f| < 10000\), and \(H(f) = 0\) for \(|f| > 10000\). What is the minimum required sampling frequency and the value of K for perfect reconstruction?

Solution

  1. Nyquist Rate (Minimum sampling frequency):
    $$ f_s = 2W = 2(6000) = \mathbf{12000 \text{ Hz}} $$
  2. With 2000 Hz Guard Band:
    $$ f_s = 2W + W_G = 12000 + 2000 = \mathbf{14000 \text{ Hz}} $$
  3. Filter Constraints for Perfect Reconstruction:
    Wait, what is an "Aliased Image Spectrum"?

    When you sample a signal, mathematics dictates that infinite "copies" (or images) of its original frequency spectrum are created at every multiple of the sampling frequency \(f_s\).

    • The original "baseband" signal lives between \(-W\) and \(+W\).
    • The very first "copy" is centered at \(f_s\). Its lower edge reaches down to \(f_s - W\).

    If our reconstruction filter doesn't reach zero before it hits that lower edge (\(f_s - W\)), it will accidentally pull in those duplicate high frequencies, permanently distorting our audio/data. This distortion is called aliasing.

    The filter \(H(f)\) completely stops passing frequencies at \(10000\) Hz. To avoid aliasing, the lower edge of the image spectrum must not enter the filter's non-zero region:
    $$ f_s - W \ge 10000 \implies f_s - 6000 \ge 10000 \implies \mathbf{f_s \ge 16000 \text{ Hz}} $$
  4. Filter Gain \(K\):

    When we sample a signal, the mathematical process of ideal sampling multiplies the original signal's amplitude by a scaling factor of \( f_s \) (or \( 1/T_s \)) in the frequency domain.

    To achieve perfect amplitude reconstruction, we must exactly undo this scaling. This means our reconstruction filter must have a gain \( K \) that multiplies that scaled spectrum back down to its original size (unity gain overall).

    Therefore, the filter's gain \( K \) must be exactly the inverse of the sampling scaling factor:

    $$ K = \frac{1}{\text{Scaling Factor}} = \frac{1}{f_s} = T_s $$

    Plugging in our sampling frequency \( f_s = 16000 \text{ Hz} \):

    $$ K = \frac{1}{16000} = \mathbf{62.5 \, \mu\text{s}} $$

📝 Question 2

Problem: The lowpass signal x(t) with a bandwidth of W is sampled with a sampling interval of \(T_s\), and the signal \( x_p(t) = \sum x(nT_s) p(t - nT_s) \) is reconstructed from the samples.

a) Find the Fourier transform of \(x_p(t)\).
b) Find the conditions for perfect reconstruction of x(t) from \(x_p(t)\).
c) Determine the required reconstruction filter.

Solution

  1. a) Fourier Transform \(X_p(f)\):
    Instead of jumping to the answer, let's derive it step-by-step:
    • Step 1: Relate to Ideal Sampling
      We know the ideally sampled signal is modeled as the original signal multiplied by an impulse train: \( x_\delta(t) = x(t) \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty \delta(t - nT_s) \).
    • Step 2: Express as Convolution
      The PAM signal \(x_p(t) = \sum x(nT_s) p(t - nT_s)\) is exactly equivalent to convolving the ideal sampled signal \(x_\delta(t)\) with the pulse shape \(p(t)\):
      $$ x_p(t) = x_\delta(t) * p(t) $$
    • Step 3: Transform to Frequency Domain
      Convolution in the time domain is multiplication in the frequency domain. Therefore, taking the Fourier transform of both sides yields:
      $$ X_p(f) = X_\delta(f) \cdot P(f) $$
    • Step 4: Substitute \(X_\delta(f)\)
      We know from sampling theory that the spectrum of an ideally sampled signal is the original spectrum replicated at multiples of \(f_s\) and scaled by \(1/T_s\):
      $$ X_\delta(f) = \frac{1}{T_s} \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty X\left(f - \frac{n}{T_s}\right) $$
    Substituting this into our equation from Step 3 gives us the final Fourier transform:
    $$ X_p(f) = \left[ \frac{1}{T_s} \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty X\left(f - \frac{n}{T_s}\right) \right] \cdot P(f) $$
  2. b) Conditions for Reconstruction:
    🎯Condition 1: Nyquist Rate
    We must sample at or above the Nyquist rate (\( f_s \ge 2W \)).
    💡Reason: If the sampling rate is lower than the Nyquist rate, the replicated high-frequency spectrums will overlap with the baseband spectrum. This overlapping causes permanent distortion known as aliasing, making perfect reconstruction impossible.
    🌊Condition 2: Non-Zero Pulse Spectrum
    The pulse spectrum \(P(f)\) must not be zero anywhere within the baseband region (\( |f| \le W \)).
    💡Reason: Looking at the reconstruction equation \(X(f) = X_{p, \text{baseband}}(f) \cdot \frac{T_s}{P(f)}\), we must divide by \(P(f)\) to equalize the pulse shape. If \(P(f) = 0\) at any frequency within the baseband, that specific frequency component is multiplied by zero during transmission and is permanently destroyed. We cannot recover a lost frequency by dividing by zero.
  3. c) Required Reconstruction Filter:
    To completely recover the original signal \(x(t)\), the reconstruction filter \(H_{rec}(f)\) must do two things:
    1. Isolate the baseband spectrum: It must act as an ideal lowpass filter to extract only the \(n=0\) term from the infinite sum in \(X_p(f)\) and completely reject the replicated high-frequency images (the \(n \neq 0\) terms).
    2. Equalize the pulse distortion: It must cancel out the magnitude and phase distortion introduced by the arbitrary pulse shape \(P(f)\).

    Looking at our spectrum equation from part (a) for the baseband region (\(|f| \le W\)), the only surviving term is for \(n=0\):

    $$ X_{p, \text{baseband}}(f) = \frac{1}{T_s} X(f) P(f) $$

    We pass this signal through our reconstruction filter \(H_{rec}(f)\), aiming to get our exact original signal \(X(f)\) back:

    $$ X_{p, \text{baseband}}(f) \cdot H_{rec}(f) = X(f) $$

    Substituting our baseband expression into this equation:

    $$ \left[ \frac{1}{T_s} X(f) P(f) \right] \cdot H_{rec}(f) = X(f) $$

    Solving for \(H_{rec}(f)\), we find the required frequency response within the baseband (\(|f| \le W\)):

    $$ H_{rec}(f) = \begin{cases} \frac{T_s}{P(f)} & \text{for } |f| \le W \\ 0 & \text{for } |f| > W \end{cases} $$

    *This can also be written compactly using the rectangular function: \( H_{rec}(f) = \frac{T_s}{P(f)} \text{rect}\left(\frac{f}{2W}\right) \).

Revision (3b): Quantization

📝 Questions 1 & 2

Problem: Let X(t) denote a wide-sense stationary (WSS) Gaussian process with signal power \(P_X = 10\).

a) Design a 16-level optimal uniform quantizer (Table 7.1).
b) What is the resulting distortion?
c) What is the amount of improvement in SQNR (dB) by doubling levels from 8 to 16?
d) Repeat a-c using optimal nonuniform quantizer (Table 7.2).

Solution

Given: Power \(P_X = \sigma^2 = 10 \implies \sigma = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.162 \).

Part 1: Uniform Quantizer

a) Design (N=16)

"Designing" a quantizer means explicitly defining its step size, representation levels (\(\hat{x}_i\)), and decision boundaries (\(a_i\)).

1. From Table 7.1, normalized step size: \(\Delta_{norm} = 0.3352\)

$$ \Delta = \sigma \cdot \Delta_{norm} = 3.162 \times 0.3352 = \mathbf{1.060} $$

2. Representation Levels (\(\hat{x}_i\)): Because N is even, the levels are placed symmetrically at half-integer multiples of \(\Delta\):

$$ \hat{x}_i = \pm \frac{\Delta}{2}, \pm \frac{3\Delta}{2}, \dots, \pm \frac{15\Delta}{2} $$

3. Decision Boundaries (\(a_i\)): The boundaries are exactly halfway between the representation levels (integer multiples of \(\Delta\)), bounded by \(\pm \infty\):

$$ a_i = 0, \pm \Delta, \pm 2\Delta, \dots, \pm 7\Delta, \pm \infty $$
b) Distortion

From Table 7.1: \(D_{norm} = 0.01154\)

$$ D_{uni} = 10 \times 0.01154 = \mathbf{0.1154} $$
c) Improvement (8 to 16)

Step 1: Understand the SQNR formula
The Signal-to-Quantization-Noise Ratio (SQNR) in decibels is calculated as \( 10 \log_{10}(P_X / D) \). Since distortion \( D = P_X \cdot D_{norm} \), the signal power \( P_X \) conveniently cancels out!

$$ SQNR = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{P_X}{P_X \cdot D_{norm}}\right) = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{D_{norm}}\right) $$

Step 2: Calculate SQNR for N=8 and N=16
Using the normalized distortions from Table 7.1 for a Gaussian source:

  • For \(N=8\), \(D_{norm} = 0.03744\)
  • For \(N=16\), \(D_{norm} = 0.01154\)
$$ SQNR_8 = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{0.03744}\right) = 14.27 \text{ dB} $$ $$ SQNR_{16} = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{0.01154}\right) = 19.38 \text{ dB} $$
Improvement = \(19.38 - 14.27\) = 5.11 dB

Part 2: Nonuniform Quantizer

a) Design (N=16)

Values scaled directly from Table 7.2 (N=16).

$$ \hat{x}_i = \sigma \cdot \hat{x}_{norm}, \quad a_i = \sigma \cdot a_{norm} $$
b) Distortion

From Table 7.2: \(D_{norm} = 0.009497\)

$$ D_{non} = 10 \times 0.009497 = \mathbf{0.09497} $$
c) Improvement (8 to 16)

Step 1: Use the simplified SQNR formula
As derived above, the signal power \( P_X \) cancels out, leaving us with \( SQNR = 10 \log_{10}(1 / D_{norm}) \).

Step 2: Calculate SQNR for N=8 and N=16
Using the normalized distortions from Table 7.2 (optimal nonuniform quantizer for a Gaussian source):

  • For \(N=8\), \(D_{norm} = 0.03454\)
  • For \(N=16\), \(D_{norm} = 0.009497\)
$$ SQNR_8 = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{0.03454}\right) = 14.62 \text{ dB} $$ $$ SQNR_{16} = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{0.009497}\right) = 20.22 \text{ dB} $$
Improvement = \(20.22 - 14.62\) = 5.60 dB

📝 Question 3

Problem: Two random variables X and Y are uniformly distributed on a square with vertices at (2,0), (0,2), (-2,0), (0,-2). Each is quantized using a 4-level uniform quantizer. a) Find the marginal pdf for X and Y. b) Calculate the resulting distortion and number of bits per (X, Y) pair.

Solution

a) Marginal PDF

The area of the diamond is \( \frac{1}{2} d_1 d_2 = \frac{1}{2}(4)(4) = 8 \). Thus the joint PDF is \(f_{X,Y}(x,y) = \frac{1}{8}\) inside the region.

Integrating over Y gives the marginal PDF for X:

$$ f_X(x) = \int_{-(2-|x|)}^{2-|x|} \frac{1}{8} dy = \mathbf{\frac{2-|x|}{4}} \quad \text{for } |x| \le 2 $$

By symmetry, \(f_Y(y) = \frac{2-|y|}{4}\) for \(|y| \le 2\).

b) Distortion & Bits per Pair

Range is \([-2, 2]\), \(N=4\). Step size \(\Delta = 1\). The 4 quantized levels are at \(-1.5, -0.5, 0.5, 1.5\).

Distortion for X is the mean squared error:

$$ D_X = 2 \left( \int_0^1 (x-0.5)^2 \frac{2-x}{4} dx + \int_1^2 (x-1.5)^2 \frac{2-x}{4} dx \right) $$
$$ D_X = 2 \left( 0.03125 + 0.010417 \right) = \frac{1}{12} $$

Total distortion \( D = D_X + D_Y = \frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{12} = \) \(\frac{1}{6}\) (or 0.1667).

Bits: \(v_X = \log_2(4) = 2\), \(v_Y = 2\). Total = 4 bits per pair.

Revision (3c): PCM System Parameters

📝 Question 1

Problem: \( R_X(\tau) = \frac{A^2}{2} e^{-|\tau|} \cos(2\pi f_0 \tau) \). A=6. Max magnitude is 6. a) How many quantization levels for SQNR of at least 60 dB? b) Min required bandwidth for binary PCM.

Solution

  1. Signal Power \( P_X = R_X(0) = \frac{36}{2} = 18 \). Max magnitude \( x_{max} = 6 \).
  2. a) Quantization Levels:
    We want \( SQNR \ge 60 \text{ dB} \implies 10^6 \). Using the uniform PCM formula:
    $$ SQNR = \frac{3 N^2 P_X}{x_{max}^2} \implies 10^6 \le \frac{3 N^2 (18)}{36} = 1.5 N^2 $$
    Solving for N: \( N^2 \ge 666666.67 \implies N \ge 816.49 \). Since N must be a power of 2: \( N = 2^{10} = \) 1024 levels (10 bits).
  3. b) Minimum Bandwidth:
    Minimum sampling rate \( f_s = 2W \). Bit rate \( R = 10 \times 2W = 20W \).
    $$ BW_{req} = \frac{R}{2} = \mathbf{10W \text{ Hz}} $$

📝 Question 2

Problem: Lowpass process \(W = 5 \text{ kHz}\). Triangle PDF max at 0, bounded by \([-2, 2]\) \(\implies x_{max}=2\).

a) Nyquist rate and 32 levels uniform: find SQNR and bit rate.
b) If BW=40kHz, max SQNR?
c) Guard band 2kHz, BW=40kHz, max SQNR?

Solution

  1. Determining Initial Signal Parameters:
    📏1. Maximum Amplitude (\(x_{max}\))
    • The problem states the PDF is bounded by \([-2, 2]\).
    • This means the signal physically swings between \(-2\) and \(+2\).
    • Therefore, the absolute maximum amplitude is naturally \(x_{max} = 2\).
    ⚡2. Signal Power (\(P_X\))

    Signal power is the expected value of \(x^2\), which means we integrate \(x^2 \cdot f_X(x)\). Let's break this down:

    Step A: Find the height of the triangle PDF
    • Base = \(4\) (from \(-2\) to \(2\)).
    • Total Area must equal \(1\) \(\implies \frac{1}{2} \cdot \text{base} \cdot \text{height} = 1\).
    • \(\frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 \cdot h = 1 \implies \mathbf{h = 1/2}\) (This is the peak at \(x=0\)).
    Step B: Write the equation for the right side
    • The line goes from the peak \((0, \frac{1}{2})\) down to \((2, 0)\).
    • The equation for this line is: \(f_X(x) = \frac{1}{4}(2 - x)\).
    Step C: Integrate to find power
    • Because the triangle is perfectly symmetric, we can just integrate the right side from \(0\) to \(2\) and multiply the whole thing by \(2\):
    \begin{align*} P_X &= 2 \int_{0}^{2} x^2 \left[ \frac{1}{4}(2 - x) \right] dx \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{2} (2x^2 - x^3) dx \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{2}{3}x^3 - \frac{1}{4}x^4 \right]_0^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{16}{3} - 4 \right) = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}} \end{align*}
  2. a) Nyquist & 32 Levels:
    \( f_s = 10 \text{ kHz} \), \(v=5\) bits.
    $$ SQNR = \frac{3 (32^2) (2/3)}{2^2} = 512 \implies \mathbf{27.09 \text{ dB}} $$
    Bit Rate \( R = v f_s = \mathbf{50 \text{ kbps}} \).
  3. b) Max SQNR for 40 kHz BW:
    \( R_{max} = 2 \times 40 = 80 \text{ kbps} \). \(v = 80/10 = 8\) bits. \(N=256\).
    $$ SQNR = \frac{3 (256^2) (2/3)}{4} = 32768 \implies \mathbf{45.15 \text{ dB}} $$
  4. c) Guard Band 2 kHz, 40 kHz BW:

    With a 2 kHz guard band, the new sampling rate is \( f_s = 10 + 2 = 12 \text{ kHz} \).

    The maximum allowable bit rate from the channel bandwidth is still \( R_{max} = 80 \text{ kbps} \).

    Since \( R = v \cdot f_s \), the number of bits per sample is bounded by:

    $$ v \le \frac{R_{max}}{f_s} = \frac{80}{12} = 6.66... \text{ bits} $$
    ⚠️Why round DOWN here?
    Normally, to achieve a specific quality (SQNR) target, you round up to make sure you have enough bits. However, here we are constrained by a hard Bandwidth Limit.

    If we rounded up to \(v=7\), the required bit rate would be \(R = 7 \times 12 \text{ kHz} = 84 \text{ kbps}\). This exceeds our maximum channel capacity of \(80 \text{ kbps}\), meaning the signal literally would not fit through the channel!

    When limited by bandwidth, you must always take the floor (round down) so you don't exceed the physical data rate limits of your system. Therefore, \( \mathbf{v = 6} \text{ bits} \).

    With \(v=6\), the number of levels is \(N = 2^6 = 64\). Now we calculate the maximum SQNR:

    $$ SQNR = \frac{3 (64^2) (2/3)}{4} = 2048 \implies \mathbf{33.11 \text{ dB}} $$

📝 Question 3

Problem: Gaussian process \( R_X(\tau) = 2\text{sinc}^2(10^4 \tau) \implies W = 10 \text{ kHz}, P_X = 2 \). \( x_{max} = 10 \).

a) Guard band 2.5kHz, N=128: bit rate, SQNR, min BW?
b) Min SQNR 56 dB, guard band 2.5kHz: N and min BW?
c) Total BW 93 kHz: max SQNR, \(W_G\), N, \(f_s\), transmission rate?

Solution

Determining Initial Signal Parameters:
📡1. Finding the Bandwidth (\(W\)) using Fourier Transform

The power spectral density \(S_X(f)\) is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function \(R_X(\tau)\).
We are given \( R_X(\tau) = 2\text{sinc}^2(10^4 \tau) \). Using standard Fourier transform pairs and the duality property:

$$ A \cdot \text{sinc}^2(B\tau) \overset{\mathcal{F}}{\longleftrightarrow} \frac{A}{B} \Lambda\left(\frac{f}{B}\right) $$

Where \(\Lambda\) is the unit triangle function which is non-zero for \(-1 \le x \le 1\).
Applying this to our function where \(A = 2\) and \(B = 10^4\):

$$ S_X(f) = \frac{2}{10^4} \Lambda\left(\frac{f}{10^4}\right) $$

This triangle function is non-zero for \(\left| \frac{f}{10^4} \right| \le 1 \implies -10^4 \le f \le 10^4\).
Therefore, the maximum frequency component (Bandwidth) is exactly \(W = 10^4 \text{ Hz} = 10 \text{ kHz}\).

⚡2. Finding Signal Power (\(P_X\))
  • The average power of a WSS process is found by evaluating the autocorrelation function at \(\tau = 0\).
  • \( P_X = R_X(0) = 2\text{sinc}^2(10^4 \times 0) \)
  • Since \(\text{sinc}(0) = 1\), we get \( P_X = 2(1)^2 = \mathbf{2} \).

a) Guard Band 2.5 kHz, N=128

  • Sampling Freq: \( f_s = 20 + 2.5 = \mathbf{22.5 \text{ kHz}} \)
  • Bit Rate: \( R = 7 \times 22.5 = \mathbf{157.5 \text{ kbps}} \)
  • Min BW: \( R/2 = \mathbf{78.75 \text{ kHz}} \)
$$ SQNR = \frac{3(128^2)(2)}{10^2} = 983.04 \implies \mathbf{29.93 \text{ dB}} $$

b) Target SQNR \(\ge\) 56 dB

  • Target SQNR: \( 10^{5.6} = 398107 \)
  • New SQNR: \(\frac{3(4096^2)(2)}{100} = \mathbf{60.03 \text{ dB}}\)
  • Min BW: \( \frac{12 \times 22.5}{2} = \mathbf{135 \text{ kHz}} \)
$$ 398107 \le \frac{3 N^2 (2)}{100} \implies \mathbf{N = 4096 \text{ (12 bits)}} $$

c) Max BW Constraint: 93 kHz

Initial setup: Max allowed bit rate is \( R_{max} = 2 \times B_T = 2 \times 93 = \mathbf{186 \text{ kbps}} \)
1️⃣Step 1: Maximize bits (\(v\))
To get the maximum SQNR, we must squeeze in the absolute maximum number of bits \(v\). Since \( v \le R_{max} / f_s \), we evaluate this at the smallest possible \(f_s\) (the Nyquist rate of 20 kHz with no guard band) to find the theoretical upper bound.
$$ v \le 186 / 20 = 9.3 \implies \mathbf{v = 9 \text{ bits}} \text{ (N=512)} $$
2️⃣Step 2: Calculate actual \(f_s\) and Guard Band (\(W_G\))
Now that we locked in \(v=9\), we find the maximum actual sampling frequency we can support without exceeding the 186 kbps limit.
Sampling Freq:
\( f_s = 186 / 9 = \mathbf{20.667 \text{ kHz}} \)
Guard Band:
\( W_G = f_s - 2W = 20.667 - 20 = \mathbf{0.667 \text{ kHz}} \)
Transmission Rate:
\( R = v \cdot f_s = \mathbf{186 \text{ kbps}} \)
3️⃣Step 3: Calculate Max SQNR
$$ \text{Max } SQNR = \frac{3(512^2)(2)}{100} = 15728.64 \implies \mathbf{41.97 \text{ dB}} $$
