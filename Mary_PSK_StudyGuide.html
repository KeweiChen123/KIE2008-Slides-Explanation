1. The Core Concept

Physical Intuition

  • No Amplitude Changes: Unlike ASK, PSK keeps the wave's height (amplitude) perfectly constant at all times.
  • Data via Phase: Data is encoded entirely by shifting the phase (delaying or advancing the carrier wave).
  • Constant Power: Because the amplitude is fixed, the transmission power and signal energy remain absolutely constant.

Signal Space Geometry

  • Same Energy: All transmitted symbols have identical energy \( \mathcal{E}_s \).
  • Circular Mapping: Therefore, signals lie perfectly on a circle with radius \( \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \).
  • Equal Spacing: To maximize protection against noise, the phases are spaced equally by exactly \( \frac{2\pi}{M} \) radians.

2. Mathematical Form

General M-ary PSK Equation

\( u_m(t) = p(t) \cos\left(2\pi f_c t + \phi_m\right) \)

  • \( p(t) \): Baseband pulse shape
  • \( f_c \): Carrier frequency
  • \( \phi_m \): Phase shift = \( \frac{2\pi m}{M} \)
  • \( m \): Symbol index \( 0, 1, \dots, M-1 \)

The Rectangular Pulse Case

  • If we use a standard rectangular pulse shape \( g_T(t) \) of duration \( T \)...
  • ...we must meticulously scale its amplitude to ensure the symbol energy perfectly equals \( \mathcal{E}_s \).
Amplitude must be exactly: \( \sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_s}{T}} \)

So the explicit equation becomes:

\( u_m(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_s}{T}} \cos\left(2\pi f_c t + \frac{2\pi m}{M}\right) \)

3. Quadrature PSK (QPSK) & Cartesian Form

Energy per Bit vs Symbol

  • Symbols group bits: M-ary PSK packs \( \log_2(M) \) bits into one symbol.
  • Formula: \( \mathcal{E}_b = \frac{\mathcal{E}_s}{\log_2(M)} \)
  • Example (QPSK): \( M=4 \), so \( \log_2(4) = 2 \) bits per symbol. Therefore, \( \mathcal{E}_b = \frac{\mathcal{E}_s}{2} \).

QPSK Split (I & Q)

  • Using the trig identity \( \cos(A+B) = \cos A \cos B - \sin A \sin B \)...
  • ...QPSK (\(M=4\)) splits perfectly into two orthogonal waves:
\( u_m(t) = g_T(t) \left[ A_{mc}\cos(2\pi f_c t) - A_{ms}\sin(2\pi f_c t) \right] \)

Where \( A_{mc} = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi m}{4}\right) \) and \( A_{ms} = \sin\left(\frac{2\pi m}{4}\right) \).

QPSK 4-States Breakdown

By plugging in \( m = 0, 1, 2, 3 \), the signal simplifies beautifully:

\( m \) Phase (\( \phi_m \)) Resulting Signal \( u_m(t) \)
0 0   \( g_T(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) \)
1 \( \frac{\pi}{2} \) (90°) -\( g_T(t) \sin(2\pi f_c t) \)
2 \( \pi \) (180°) -\( g_T(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) \)
3 \( \frac{3\pi}{2} \) (270°)   \( g_T(t) \sin(2\pi f_c t) \)

4. Vector Space & Basis Functions

2D Vector Representation

Digital phase-modulated signals can be geometrically represented as two-dimensional vectors in the In-Phase (I) and Quadrature (Q) plane.

\( \mathbf{s}_m = \left[ \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \cos\left(\frac{2\pi m}{M}\right), \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi m}{M}\right) \right] \)

This directly expands to our Cartesian amplitudes:

\( \mathbf{s}_m = \left[ A_{mc}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s}, \ A_{ms}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \right] \).

Orthonormal Basis Functions

Any PSK signal can be built using two perpendicular (orthogonal) building blocks of unit energy:

  • In-Phase (\( \psi_1 \)):
    \( \psi_1(t) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}_s}} g_T(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) \)
  • Quadrature (\( \psi_2 \)):
    \( \psi_2(t) = -\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}_s}} g_T(t) \sin(2\pi f_c t) \)

Note: The negative sign on \( \psi_2 \) matches the standard \( \cos(A+B) \) expansion. The energy of the pulse \( g_T(t) \) is normalized to \( 2\mathcal{E}_s \).

Binary PSK vs. Binary ASK

  • The Setup: For Binary PSK (BPSK, \( M=2 \)), the two possible phases are exactly \( 0 \) and \( \pi \).
  • 1D Axis: This forces the symbols to lie exactly at \( +\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \) and \( -\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \) on a single, straight one-dimensional line.
  • The Big Takeaway: Mathematically and geometrically, Binary PSK is essentially identical to a bipolar Binary ASK (also called antipodal signaling).

5. Bit Mapping & Gray Encoding

Mapping \( k \) Bits

  • Bits to Phases: An M-ary PSK system groups \( k \) bits together to form \( M = 2^k \) unique symbols (phases).
  • The Challenge: We have \( M \) different bit combinations (e.g., 00, 01, 10, 11). How do we assign each specific binary pattern to a phase angle around the circle?

The Gray Code Advantage

Gray Encoding is the industry standard for mapping bits because adjacent phases differ by only one binary digit.

Why it matters: The most likely error caused by noise is mistaking a phase for its nearest neighbor. With Gray Code, if you mistakenly decode the adjacent symbol, you only suffer a 1-bit error instead of a multi-bit error!

6. Euclidean Distance & Error Performance

Distance Between Any Two Points

The Euclidean distance \( d_{mn} \) between any two signals \( \mathbf{s}_m \) and \( \mathbf{s}_n \) in the constellation space is critical for error analysis:

\( d_{mn} = \sqrt{2\mathcal{E}_s \left[ 1 - \cos\left(\frac{2\pi(m-n)}{M}\right) \right]} \)

Derived using the Law of Cosines on the signal space circle of radius \( \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \).

Minimum Distance (\( d_{min} \))

The distance between adjacent points (where \( m-n = 1 \)) determines the worst-case error performance in Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN):

\( d_{min} = 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{M}\right) \)
  • Larger \( d_{min} \) = Stronger noise immunity.
  • As \( M \) increases, \( \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{M}\right) \) decreases, causing points to pack closer together and error rates to spike.

7. Error Probability & Performance

Probability of Symbol Error (\( P_M \))

The error probability depends heavily on the \( Q \)-function (the tail probability of the Gaussian noise distribution).

  • BPSK (\( M=2 \)):
    \( P_2 = Q\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{N_0}}\right) \)
  • QPSK (\( M=4 \)):
    \( P_4 \approx 2Q\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{N_0}}\right) \)
  • M-ary PSK (Large \( M \)):
    \( P_M \approx \frac{2(M-1)}{M} Q\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\pi^2 \log_2(M) \mathcal{E}_b}{M^2 N_0}}\right) \)

The 6dB Performance Penalty

For large values of \( M \), if you want to double the number of phases (e.g., jump from 8-PSK to 16-PSK) while maintaining the exact same error performance, you must increase the signal power.

Rule of Thumb: Doubling \( M \) requires an additional 6 dB/bit of energy to offset the closer spacing of the constellation points.

This perfectly illustrates the fundamental trade-off in digital communications: you can transmit more bits per symbol, but it requires significantly more power to do so reliably.

8. Example Walkthrough: Distance vs Energy

Exam Question: BPSK vs QPSK vs 8-PSK

  • Problem: Consider the \( M=2, 4 \) and \( 8 \) PSK signal constellations. All have the exact same transmitted signal energy \( \mathcal{E}_s \).
  • Part 1: Determine the minimum distance \( d_{min} \) between adjacent signal points for each.
  • Part 2: For \( M=8 \), determine by how many dB the transmitted signal energy \( \mathcal{E}_s \) must be increased to achieve the same \( d_{min} \) as \( M=4 \).

Step-by-Step Solution

Part 1: Finding \( d_{min} \)
Using the formula \( d_{min} = 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{M}\right) \):

  • BPSK (\( M=2 \)): \( \ d_{min} = 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \)
  • QPSK (\( M=4 \)): \( \ d_{min} = 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = \sqrt{2\mathcal{E}_s} \approx 1.414\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \)
  • 8-PSK (\( M=8 \)): \( \ d_{min} = 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right) \approx 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s}(0.3827) \approx 0.765\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} \)

Part 2: The Energy Penalty for 8-PSK
We want the \( d_{min} \) of 8-PSK (let's denote its energy as \( \mathcal{E}_{s8} \)) to perfectly match the \( d_{min} \) of QPSK (energy \( \mathcal{E}_{s4} \)).

\( 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{s8}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right) = 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{s4}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) \)

Solving for the ratio of their energies:

\( \frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{s8}}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{s4}}} = \frac{\sin(\pi/4)}{\sin(\pi/8)} = \frac{1/\sqrt{2}}{0.3827} \approx 1.8477 \)

Squaring both sides gives us the energy ratio: \( \frac{\mathcal{E}_{s8}}{\mathcal{E}_{s4}} \approx (1.8477)^2 \approx 3.414 \)

Finally, convert this ratio to Decibels (dB):

\( 10 \log_{10}(3.414) \approx \mathbf{5.33 \ dB} \)

Conclusion: You must pump 5.33 dB more power into an 8-PSK system just to maintain the same distance between points (and therefore the same error protection) as a standard QPSK system!

9. Exam Traps & Misconceptions

Missing the Square Root

Mistake: Writing amplitude as \( \frac{2\mathcal{E}_s}{T} \).

Correction: Energy is proportional to voltage squared. The amplitude must be exactly \( \sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_s}{T}} \).

Energy vs Power

Mistake: Confusing Symbol Energy (\( \mathcal{E}_s \)) with Power (\( P \)).

Correction: Power is Energy over Time (\( P = \frac{\mathcal{E}_s}{T} \)). Faster transmission (smaller \( T \)) requires MORE power to maintain the same symbol energy.

Distance vs Energy

Mistake: Thinking \( d_{min} \) is completely independent of the alphabet size \( M \).

Correction: While distance increases with energy \( \mathcal{E}_s \), it aggressively decreases as \( M \) increases because of the \( \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{M}\right) \) term.
