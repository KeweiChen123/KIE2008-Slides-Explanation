Chapter 1: The Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization Procedure

💡 Physical Intuition First

In digital communication, we send digital information using analog signal waveforms (like a pulse or a sine wave). If we have $M$ possible signal waveforms, analyzing them in the time domain gets incredibly complicated.

Imagine trying to describe locations in a room without an XYZ coordinate system. It would be a mess! The Gram-Schmidt process is our tool to build a neat "XYZ coordinate system" for our signals. It takes our set of messy, overlapping signal waveforms and extracts a set of pure, mutually perpendicular (orthogonal) basic building blocks called basis functions. Once we have these basis functions, any signal we want to transmit can simply be built by adding up certain amounts of each basis function.

The core idea of Gram-Schmidt is projection. To find a new perpendicular direction, we take a signal, see how much of it overlaps with our existing directions (projection), and subtract that overlap. 📐 Mathematical Derivations

Assume we have a set of $M$ signal waveforms $s_m(t)$ for $1 \le m \le M$. We want to construct $N \le M$ orthonormal basis functions $\psi_n(t)$.

Step 1: The first basis function $\psi_1(t)$

We simply take the first signal $s_1(t)$ and normalize it to have unit energy.

Calculate energy: $$\mathcal{E}_1 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s_1^2(t) dt$$

Normalize: $$\psi_1(t) = \frac{s_1(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_1}}$$

Step 2: The second basis function $\psi_2(t)$

Find the projection (overlap) of $s_2(t)$ onto $\psi_1(t)$: $$c_{21} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s_2(t)\psi_1(t)dt$$

Subtract the overlap to find the orthogonal piece $d_2(t)$: $$d_2(t) = s_2(t) - c_{21}\psi_1(t)$$

Normalize it: $$\psi_2(t) = \frac{d_2(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_2}}$$ where $$\mathcal{E}_2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} d_2^2(t) dt$$

General Step $k$:

For the $k$-th function, subtract its projections onto all previously found basis functions:

$$d_k(t) = s_k(t) - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} c_{ki}\psi_i(t)$$

Example ($k=3$): \(d_3(t) = s_3(t) - c_{31}\psi_1(t) - c_{32}\psi_2(t)\)

where $$c_{ki} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s_k(t)\psi_i(t)dt$$ for $i = 1, 2, \dots, k-1$.

Example ($k=3$): \(c_{31} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s_3(t)\psi_1(t)dt\), and \(c_{32} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s_3(t)\psi_2(t)dt\)

Then normalize: $$\psi_k(t) = \frac{d_k(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_k}}$$ If $d_k(t) = 0$, it means the signal was totally dependent on previous ones, so no new basis function is created.

✏️ Worked Example: Gram-Schmidt

Problem: Given two signals over the interval $0 \le t \le 2$:

  • $s_1(t) = 1$ for $0 \le t \le 2$
  • $s_2(t) = 1$ for $0 \le t \le 1$, and $s_2(t) = -1$ for $1 < t \le 2$

Find the orthonormal basis functions $\psi_1(t)$ and $\psi_2(t)$.

Solution:

Step 1: Find $\psi_1(t)$

Energy of $s_1$: $$\mathcal{E}_1 = \int_0^2 (1)^2 dt = [t]_0^2 = 2$$

$$\psi_1(t) = \frac{s_1(t)}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \quad \text{for } 0 \le t \le 2$$

Step 2: Find $\psi_2(t)$

Calculate the projection coefficient $c_{21}$:

$$c_{21} = \int_0^2 s_2(t)\psi_1(t) dt = \int_0^1 (1)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) dt + \int_1^2 (-1)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) dt = 0$$

Because the projection is $0$, $s_2(t)$ is already orthogonal to $\psi_1(t)$!

So, $$d_2(t) = s_2(t) - 0 = s_2(t)$$

Energy of $d_2$: $$\mathcal{E}_2 = \int_0^1 (1)^2 dt + \int_1^2 (-1)^2 dt = 1 + 1 = 2$$

$$\psi_2(t) = \frac{d_2(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_2}} = \frac{s_2(t)}{\sqrt{2}}$$

Chapter 2: Geometric Representation in Signal Space

💡 Physical Intuition First

Now that we have our basis functions (our "XYZ axes"), we can map every single signal waveform directly to a point (or vector) in a geometric space. Instead of dealing with continuous waves over time, we just deal with coordinates like $(2.5, -1.0)$.

This is incredibly powerful because the messy realities of the channel (like additive white Gaussian noise, AWGN) become simple geometry problems. To figure out if a signal was received correctly, the receiver just looks for the "closest point" in the signal space. Euclidean distance becomes the ultimate metric for error probability!

📐 Mathematical Derivations

Vector Representation:

Any signal $s_m(t)$ can be expressed as a linear combination of the orthonormal basis functions:

$$s_m(t) = \sum_{n=1}^N s_{mn} \psi_n(t)$$

Example: \(s_1(t) = s_{11}\psi_1(t) + s_{12}\psi_2(t)\)

The coefficients (coordinates) are found via the inner product:

$$s_{mn} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s_m(t)\psi_n(t) dt$$

Example ($m=2, n=1$): \(s_{21} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s_2(t)\psi_1(t) dt\)

So, the signal can be mapped exactly to a vector of coordinates: $\mathbf{s}_m = [s_{m1}, s_{m2}, \dots, s_{mN}]$.

Equivalence Example ($N=3$): The vector representation \(\mathbf{s}_m = [s_{m1}, s_{m2}, s_{m3}]\) is mathematically and physically equivalent to the time-domain waveform \(s_m(t) = s_{m1}\psi_1(t) + s_{m2}\psi_2(t) + s_{m3}\psi_3(t)\).

Energy and Distance:

The energy of the signal waveform is identical to the squared length of its vector (Parseval's theorem equivalent):

$$\mathcal{E}_m = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s_m^2(t) dt = \sum_{n=1}^N s_{mn}^2 = \|\mathbf{s}_m\|^2$$

The distance between any two signals is simply the Euclidean distance:

$$d_{mn}^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} [s_m(t) - s_n(t)]^2 dt = \|\mathbf{s}_m - \mathbf{s}_n\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^N (s_{mi} - s_{ni})^2$$

✏️ Worked Example: Vector Coordinates

Problem: Using the signals and basis functions from the previous example, find the vector representation of $s_1(t)$ and calculate its energy using the vector method.

Solution:

We know $\psi_1(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $\psi_2(t) = \frac{s_2(t)}{\sqrt{2}}$.

Since $s_1(t) = 1$, it is exactly $\sqrt{2} \times \psi_1(t)$.

Coordinate 1: $$s_{11} = \int_0^2 s_1(t)\psi_1(t) dt = \int_0^2 (1)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) dt = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}$$

Coordinate 2: $$s_{12} = \int_0^2 s_1(t)\psi_2(t) dt = 0 \quad \text{(because they are orthogonal)}$$

So, the vector is $\mathbf{s}_1 = [\sqrt{2}, 0]$.

Energy Calculation:

$$\mathcal{E}_1 = \|\mathbf{s}_1\|^2 = (\sqrt{2})^2 + (0)^2 = 2$$

This perfectly matches our time-domain calculation!

Exam Traps & Misconceptions

Gram-Schmidt and signal spaces are notorious for a few classic exam tricks. Don't fall for them!

🚨

Trap 1: The Dimension N is always equal to the number of Signals M

✅
The dimension $N$ is always less than or equal to $M$ ($N \le M$).

You only get a new dimension (basis function) if a signal introduces something completely new. If $s_3(t)$ is just $s_1(t) + s_2(t)$ (linearly dependent), it carries no "new" direction. The Gram-Schmidt process will yield $d_3(t) = 0$, meaning no $\psi_3(t)$ is created. $N$ only equals $M$ when every single signal in the set is linearly independent of the others.

🚨

Trap 2: A different basis set means your system is not optimized

✅
The set of orthonormal basis functions $\{\psi_n(t)\}$ is not unique, and any valid basis works perfectly!

The basis functions generated by Gram-Schmidt depend entirely on the order in which you input the signals. Starting the process with $s_4$ instead of $s_1$ will give you a completely different set of $\psi$ functions. Think of it like choosing coordinate axes: measuring a physical room relative to the North wall versus the East wall gives different coordinates, but the room itself (and the distances between objects) remains exactly the same. The geometric relationships (distances, error probability) remain perfectly identical regardless of the basis.

🚨

Trap 3: $\mathcal{E}_k$ is the energy of the actual signal $s_k(t)$

✅
$\mathcal{E}_k$ computed during Gram-Schmidt is the energy of the intermediate orthogonal piece $d_k(t)$, not $s_k(t)$.

During the procedure, after subtracting the projections from $s_3(t)$, you are left with a residual signal $d_3(t)$. It is this residual signal that you calculate the energy for ($\mathcal{E}_3$) in order to normalize it into $\psi_3$.

Distance Rule: If you want the actual energy of $s_k(t)$, you compute $\int s_k^2(t) dt$ or find its squared distance from the origin in the signal space: $\|\mathbf{s}_k\|^2 = \sum s_{ki}^2$.

🚨

Trap 4: Mixing up a signal's Amplitude with its Energy

✅
A rectangular pulse with an amplitude of 2 does NOT have an energy of 2.

Energy is the integral of the squared signal over its entire duration ($\int s^2(t) dt$). For a rectangular pulse of amplitude $A$ and duration $T$, the energy is actually $A^2 \times T$. You must always account for both the amplitude squared and the duration of the signal (like the bit interval $T_b$). Forgetting to square the amplitude or multiply by $T$ is the #1 cause of calculation errors in exams!

Key Terms Glossary

  • AWGN (Additive White Gaussian Noise): The simplest mathematical model for channel noise, assuming noise is added to the signal and its power is spread evenly across all frequencies.
  • Basis Functions ($\psi$): A set of basic, non-overlapping (orthogonal) functions of unit energy that act as the axes of the signal space.
  • Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization: A mathematical procedure to convert a set of overlapping vectors (or signals) into a set of orthogonal basis vectors.
  • Linear Independence: A condition where no signal in a set can be created by simply scaling and adding the other signals together.
  • Orthogonal: Mutually perpendicular. In signals, it means their inner product (integral of their product) is exactly zero.
  • Signal Space: An $N$-dimensional geometric space where every point represents a unique signal waveform.

📌 Summary & Revision Notes

  • Any set of $M$ signals can be represented geometrically in a signal space of dimension $N \le M$.
  • The Gram-Schmidt procedure is used to systematically find the $N$ orthonormal basis functions. It relies on finding projections and subtracting them.
  • Once the basis is found, signals lose their complex time-domain representation and become simple vectors $\mathbf{s}_m = [s_{m1}, s_{m2}, \dots, s_{mN}]$.
  • This vector representation drastically simplifies analysis. The energy of a signal is its distance from the origin squared. The difference between two signals is the Euclidean distance between their points.
  • Communication channels can now be analyzed as vector channels rather than continuous waveform channels, simplifying optimum receiver design.
