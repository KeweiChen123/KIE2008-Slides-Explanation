Section 1

Concept Explanation

The physical story

An analog communication signal is like a continuous hand-drawn curve. It has a value at every instant, which is beautiful but awkward for a digital machine: there are infinitely many instants and infinitely many possible amplitudes.

Analog-to-digital conversion makes two controlled sacrifices. First, it only looks at the signal at regularly spaced instants. Second, it rounds each measured amplitude to one of a finite set of allowed values.

The design question is not "Can we avoid all loss?" The question is "How much sampling rate, how many quantization levels, and how much bit rate are needed so the loss is acceptable?"

The three operations

  1. Sampling: converts \(x(t)\) into a sequence \(x(nT_s)\). Time becomes discrete, but amplitude is still continuous.
  2. Quantization: maps each sample to \(Q(x)\). Amplitude becomes discrete, so the sequence can be represented by a finite number of bits.
  3. Encoding: assigns a binary word to each quantization level. If \(N = 2^v\), each sample uses \(v\) bits.
Time

Sampling asks: when do we look?

Pick sample times close enough together that the waveform cannot hide fast changes between samples.

\[ f_s \ge 2W \]
Amplitude

Quantization asks: how precise?

Round every sample to one of \(N\) allowed levels. More levels mean smaller error, but more bits.

\[ D=E[(X-Q(X))^2] \]
Bits

Encoding asks: what name?

Give each level a binary label. Once \(N=2^v\), every sample needs \(v\) bits.

\[ R=vf_s \]

Sampling intuition

Sampling is like taking evenly spaced photographs of a moving object. Frequent photos preserve the motion; sparse photos can make fast motion look slow.

The clean rule is \(f_s\ge2W\). In practice, a guard band gives real filters room to roll off.

Quantization intuition

Quantization replaces a smooth amplitude ruler with a staircase. Every input inside one region is rounded to the same output level.

More steps reduce the rounding error. Fewer steps reduce the bit rate but increase distortion.

Waveform encoding intuition

PCM encodes the waveform directly. Nonuniform PCM reshapes amplitudes first, which is useful for speech-like signals.

DPCM encodes differences. Delta modulation is a one-bit version, and ADM changes the step size when the slope changes.

Sampling controls time

Main design variable: \(f_s\). Too low causes aliasing; higher rates cost more samples per second.

Quantization controls amplitude

Main design variable: \(N\) or \(v\). More levels reduce distortion but increase the bit rate.

Encoding controls bits

Main design variable: \(R = vf_s\). This links source quality to transmission bandwidth.

1. Sampling theorem and spectrum replication

Assume \(x(t)\) is bandlimited to \(W\), meaning \(X(f) = 0\) for \(|f| \ge W\). Sampling with impulse train spacing \(T_s\) gives:

\[ x_\delta(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x(nT_s)\delta(t-nT_s) = x(t)\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-nT_s) \]

The Fourier transform of an impulse train is another impulse train:

\[ \mathcal{F}\left\{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-nT_s)\right\} = \frac{1}{T_s}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta\left(f-\frac{n}{T_s}\right) \]

Multiplication in time becomes convolution in frequency:

\[ X_\delta(f) = X(f) * \frac{1}{T_s}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta\left(f-\frac{n}{T_s}\right) = \frac{1}{T_s}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}X\left(f-\frac{n}{T_s}\right) \]

This is the key idea: sampling creates shifted replicas of the original spectrum every \(f_s = 1/T_s\) Hz.

For perfect reconstruction, the replicas must not overlap. Since the original spectrum occupies \(-W\) to \(+W\), the spacing must satisfy \(f_s \ge 2W\).

Missing steps made explicit

  1. Define the sampling impulse train as \(p(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-nT_s)\).
  2. Multiplying by \(p(t)\) keeps only the values of \(x(t)\) at the impulse locations.
\[ x(t)\delta(t-nT_s)=x(nT_s)\delta(t-nT_s) \] \[ x_\delta(t)=x(t)p(t) =\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x(nT_s)\delta(t-nT_s) \]
  1. The Fourier transform of the sampling train is found from its Fourier-series expansion.
\[ p(t)=\frac{1}{T_s}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{j2\pi nt/T_s} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(f)=\frac{1}{T_s}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta\left(f-\frac{n}{T_s}\right) \]
  1. Now substitute \(P(f)\) into \(X_\delta(f)=X(f)*P(f)\) and use the shifting property of convolution with an impulse.
\[ X(f)*\delta(f-f_0)=X(f-f_0) \] \[ X_\delta(f) =\frac{1}{T_s}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \left[X(f)*\delta\left(f-\frac{n}{T_s}\right)\right] =\frac{1}{T_s}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} X\left(f-\frac{n}{T_s}\right) \]
  1. For no aliasing, the baseband copy ending at \(W\) must not touch the first positive shifted copy starting at \(f_s-W\).
\[ W\le f_s-W \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_s\ge2W \]

2. Reconstruction formula

At the Nyquist condition or above, an ideal lowpass reconstruction filter keeps the central spectrum and removes the shifted replicas. The time-domain interpolation formula is:

\[ x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x(nT_s)\operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{t}{T_s}-n\right) \]

Using the lecture's bandwidth notation \(T_s = 1/(2W)\), this is equivalently:

\[ x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x(nT_s)\operatorname{sinc}\left(2Wt-n\right) \]

Where the sinc pulse comes from

  1. The sampled spectrum has a scale factor \(1/T_s\), so the ideal reconstruction filter must multiply the central copy by \(T_s\).
\[ H(f)= \begin{cases} T_s, & |f|\le W\\ 0, & |f|>W \end{cases} \]
  1. Take the inverse Fourier transform of the ideal lowpass rectangle.
\[ h(t)=T_s\int_{-W}^{W}e^{j2\pi ft}\,df =T_s\left[\frac{e^{j2\pi ft}}{j2\pi t}\right]_{-W}^{W} \] \[ =T_s\frac{e^{j2\pi Wt}-e^{-j2\pi Wt}}{j2\pi t} =T_s\frac{2j\sin(2\pi Wt)}{j2\pi t} \]
  1. At the Nyquist rate \(T_s=1/(2W)\), so \(T_s(2W)=1\).
\[ h(t)=T_s(2W)\frac{\sin(2\pi Wt)}{2\pi Wt} =\operatorname{sinc}(2Wt) \]
  1. Filtering each weighted impulse shifts the impulse response to that sample time.
\[ x_r(t)=x_\delta(t)*h(t) =\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x(nT_s)\left[\delta(t-nT_s)*h(t)\right] \] \[ =\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x(nT_s)h(t-nT_s) =\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x(nT_s)\operatorname{sinc}\left(2W(t-nT_s)\right) \]

The sinc pulse is the mathematically ideal interpolation pulse. Linear interpolation is easier to draw, but sinc interpolation is what gives exact reconstruction under the theorem's assumptions.

3. Quantization distortion and SQNR

A scalar quantizer maps every input value \(x\) in region \(\mathcal{R}_i\) to a reproduction level \(\hat{x}_i\):

\[ Q(x) = \hat{x}_i,\quad x \in \mathcal{R}_i \]

The instantaneous squared error is:

\[ d(x,\hat{x}) = [x-Q(x)]^2 = \tilde{x}^2 \]

For a random input \(X\), the average distortion is mean squared error:

\[ D = E\left[(X-Q(X))^2\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{R}_i}(x-\hat{x}_i)^2f_X(x)\,dx \]

Signal-to-quantization-noise ratio compares useful signal power to quantization distortion:

\[ SQNR = \frac{E[X^2]}{D} = \frac{P_X}{D} \]

Region-by-region distortion expansion

For an \(N\)-level scalar quantizer, the regions are usually written as \(\mathcal{R}_1=(-\infty,a_1]\), \(\mathcal{R}_i=(a_{i-1},a_i]\), and \(\mathcal{R}_N=(a_{N-1},\infty)\). Therefore the expectation expands into one integral per region:

\[ D= \int_{-\infty}^{a_1}(x-\hat{x}_1)^2f_X(x)\,dx +\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\int_{a_{i-1}}^{a_i}(x-\hat{x}_i)^2f_X(x)\,dx +\int_{a_{N-1}}^{\infty}(x-\hat{x}_N)^2f_X(x)\,dx \]
  1. If the problem gives \(a_i\), \(\hat{x}_i\), and \(f_X(x)\), plug them directly into these integrals.
  2. If the problem gives a table value for normalized Gaussian distortion, scale it by the actual variance: \(D_{actual}=\sigma^2D_{table}\).
  3. Convert SQNR to decibels only after computing the ratio: \(SQNR_{dB}=10\log_{10}(P_X/D)\).

4. Uniform PCM SQNR

For uniform PCM with input range \([-x_{max},x_{max}]\) and \(N=2^v\) levels, the step size is:

\[ \Delta = \frac{2x_{max}}{N} = \frac{x_{max}}{2^{v-1}} \]

With many small quantization intervals, the quantization error is modeled as uniform on \((-\Delta/2,\Delta/2]\), so:

\[ D = E[\tilde{X}^2] = \frac{\Delta^2}{12} = \frac{x_{max}^2}{3N^2} \]

Do not skip the noise-power integral

The \(\Delta^2/12\) result comes from treating the quantization error \(\tilde{X}\) as uniformly distributed over one quantization cell:

\[ f_{\tilde{X}}(\tilde{x})= \begin{cases} \frac{1}{\Delta}, & -\frac{\Delta}{2}<\tilde{x}\le\frac{\Delta}{2}\\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \] \[ E[\tilde{X}^2] =\int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2}\tilde{x}^2\frac{1}{\Delta}\,d\tilde{x} =\frac{1}{\Delta}\left[\frac{\tilde{x}^3}{3}\right]_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2} \] \[ =\frac{1}{\Delta}\left(\frac{\Delta^3}{24}-\left[-\frac{\Delta^3}{24}\right]\right) =\frac{\Delta^2}{12} \]

Therefore:

\[ SQNR = \frac{P_X}{D} = \frac{3N^2P_X}{x_{max}^2} = \frac{3\cdot 4^v P_X}{x_{max}^2} \]

In decibels, using \(N=2^v\):

\[ SQNR_{dB} =10\log_{10}\left(\frac{3\cdot4^vP_X}{x_{max}^2}\right) \] \[ =10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_X}{x_{max}^2}\right) +10\log_{10}(3) +10\log_{10}(4^v) \] \[ \approx 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_X}{x_{max}^2}\right)+4.8+6v \]

This is why each extra bit improves SQNR by about 6 dB.

5. Optimal nonuniform quantizer conditions

For mean squared error, the optimal boundary between two adjacent reproduction levels sits halfway between those levels:

\[ a_i = \frac{\hat{x}_i+\hat{x}_{i+1}}{2} \]

That midpoint rule comes from differentiating the two distortion terms that touch boundary \(a_i\):

\[ D_i=\int_{a_{i-1}}^{a_i}(x-\hat{x}_i)^2f_X(x)\,dx +\int_{a_i}^{a_{i+1}}(x-\hat{x}_{i+1})^2f_X(x)\,dx \] \[ \frac{\partial D_i}{\partial a_i} =(a_i-\hat{x}_i)^2f_X(a_i) -(a_i-\hat{x}_{i+1})^2f_X(a_i) \] \[ \frac{\partial D_i}{\partial a_i}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (a_i-\hat{x}_i)^2=(a_i-\hat{x}_{i+1})^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_i=\frac{\hat{x}_i+\hat{x}_{i+1}}{2} \]

For a fixed region, the best reproduction level is the conditional mean of the input inside that region:

\[ \hat{x}_i = \frac{\int_{a_{i-1}}^{a_i} x f_X(x)\,dx} {\int_{a_{i-1}}^{a_i} f_X(x)\,dx} \]

The centroid rule comes from differentiating the region distortion with respect to \(\hat{x}_i\):

\[ D_i=\int_{a_{i-1}}^{a_i}(x-\hat{x}_i)^2f_X(x)\,dx \] \[ \frac{\partial D_i}{\partial \hat{x}_i} =\int_{a_{i-1}}^{a_i}-2(x-\hat{x}_i)f_X(x)\,dx=0 \] \[ \int_{a_{i-1}}^{a_i}xf_X(x)\,dx =\hat{x}_i\int_{a_{i-1}}^{a_i}f_X(x)\,dx \] \[ \therefore\quad \hat{x}_i = \frac{\int_{a_{i-1}}^{a_i}xf_X(x)\,dx} {\int_{a_{i-1}}^{a_i}f_X(x)\,dx} \]

The lecture describes the standard alternating method: start with regions, compute reproduction values, update boundaries, and repeat until distortion changes very little.

6. Bit rate and transmission bandwidth

If there are \(N = 2^v\) quantization levels, each sample uses \(v=\log_2N\) bits. With \(f_s\) samples per second:

\[ R = vf_s \quad \text{bits/s} \]
  1. Bandwidth gives the minimum sampling rate: \(f_s=2W\), or \(f_s=2W+W_G\) if a guard band is specified.
  2. Quantization levels give bits per sample: \(v=\log_2N\).
  3. Multiplying those two gives bit rate: \(R=vf_s\).

For binary baseband transmission in the lecture examples, the minimum transmission bandwidth is taken as:

\[ BW_T = \frac{R}{2} = \frac{vf_s}{2} \]
This is why a single design change can ripple through the whole system: increasing \(N\) improves SQNR, but it increases \(v\), which increases \(R\), which increases the required transmission bandwidth.

7. Companding laws, DPCM, DM, and ADM

The lecture gives two common compressor functions for speech coding:

\[ g_\mu(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)\frac{\log(1+\mu |x|)}{\log(1+\mu)} \] \[ g_A(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)\frac{1+\log(A|x|)}{1+\log A} \]

DPCM quantizes a prediction error rather than the sample itself:

\[ Y_n = X_n - Y'_{n-1} \]

The receiver reconstructs by adding the quantized difference back to the same prediction:

\[ \hat{Y}_n=Q(Y_n) \] \[ Y'_n=Y'_{n-1}+\hat{Y}_n \]
  1. If adjacent samples are highly correlated, \(X_n\approx Y'_{n-1}\), so \(Y_n\) has smaller dynamic range than \(X_n\).
  2. A smaller dynamic range can be quantized with fewer bits for similar distortion.
  3. That is the core reason DPCM can beat direct PCM at lower bit rates.

Delta modulation uses only a one-bit quantizer with output \(\pm\Delta\). The staircase estimate is updated by moving one step up or down:

\[ e_n=x(nT_s)-\hat{x}_{n-1} \] \[ b_n=\operatorname{sgn}(e_n)\in\{+1,-1\} \] \[ \hat{x}_n=\hat{x}_{n-1}+\Delta b_n \]

Adaptive delta modulation changes \(\Delta\) based on recent quantizer signs:

\[ \Delta_n = \Delta_{n-1}K^{\epsilon_n\epsilon_{n-1}},\quad K>1 \]

If two signs are the same, \(\epsilon_n\epsilon_{n-1}=+1\), so \(\Delta_n=\Delta_{n-1}K\) and the modulator reacts faster. If the signs alternate, \(\epsilon_n\epsilon_{n-1}=-1\), so \(\Delta_n=\Delta_{n-1}/K\) and granular noise is reduced.

Section 4

Worked Examples

Find \(W\)

Bandwidth comes first because it decides the sampling rate.

Find \(v\)

Convert \(N\) levels into bits using \(v=\log_2N\).

Find power

Use PSD area, \(R_X(0)\), or the given distribution.

Finish units

Check Hz, bps, dB, and transmission bandwidth at the end.

Example 1: Guard-band sampling rate

Problem: A bandlimited signal has bandwidth \(W = 3400\) Hz. What sampling rate guarantees a guard band of \(W_G = 1200\) Hz?

  1. Start with Nyquist sampling: \(f_s = 2W\).
  2. Add the requested guard band: \(f_s = 2W + W_G\).
  3. Substitute values: \(f_s = 2(3400) + 1200 = 8000\) Hz.
\[ \boxed{f_s = 8000\ \text{samples/s}} \]

The extra 1200 Hz gives the reconstruction filter room to roll off instead of requiring an ideal vertical edge.

Example 2: Offset sampling interpolation

Problem: Samples are taken every \(T_s\), but the first sample occurs at \(0<t_0<T_s\). Determine the interpolation relation.

  1. Define a shifted signal \(y(t)=x(t+t_0)\). Its samples at \(kT_s\) are \(y(kT_s)=x(t_0+kT_s)\).
  2. Apply the sampling theorem to \(y(t)\):
\[ y(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}y(kT_s)\operatorname{sinc}\left(2W(t-kT_s)\right) \]
  1. Substitute back \(y(kT_s)=x(t_0+kT_s)\):
\[ x(t+t_0)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}x(t_0+kT_s)\operatorname{sinc}\left(2W(t-kT_s)\right) \]

This says offset samples still reconstruct the signal if they are uniformly spaced and the sampling theorem condition is satisfied.

Example 3: Gaussian source rate and distortion

Problem: A zero-mean stationary Gaussian source has \(S_X(f)=2\) for \(|f|<100\) Hz and 0 otherwise. It is sampled at Nyquist rate and quantized using 8 levels. Find the rate and distortion.

  1. The bandwidth is \(W=100\) Hz, so \(f_s=2W=200\) Hz.
  2. There are \(N=8\) levels, so \(v=\log_2 8=3\) bits/sample.
  3. The bit rate is \(R=vf_s=3(200)=600\) bps.
  4. The variance equals area under the PSD:
\[ \sigma_X^2 = \int_{-100}^{100}2\,df = 400 \]
  1. Use the given boundaries and reproduction values to integrate squared error region by region:
\[ D=\sum_{i=1}^{8}\int_{\mathcal{R}_i}(x-\hat{x}_i)^2f_X(x)\,dx \approx 33.38 \]

Answer: \(R=600\) bps and \(D\approx33.38\). The lecture reports an SQNR around \(15.23\) dB.

Example 4: Optimal uniform quantizer scaling

Problem: For the Gaussian source with \(\sigma^2=400\), design an 8-level optimal uniform quantizer using the table values \(\Delta=0.5860\) and \(D=0.03744\) for a unit-variance source.

  1. The standard deviation is \(\sigma=20\).
  2. Scale the normalized step size: \(\Delta = 20(0.5860)=11.72\).
  3. The symmetric reconstruction levels are:
\[ \hat{x}_1=-41.02,\ \hat{x}_2=-29.30,\ \hat{x}_3=-17.58,\ \hat{x}_4=-5.86 \] \[ \hat{x}_5=5.86,\ \hat{x}_6=17.58,\ \hat{x}_7=29.30,\ \hat{x}_8=41.02 \]
  1. Scale normalized distortion by variance:
\[ D = \sigma^2(0.03744)=400(0.03744)=14.98 \]

The optimal uniform quantizer improves distortion compared with the earlier fixed quantizer.

Example 5: Optimal nonuniform quantizer comparison

Problem: Use an 8-level optimal nonuniform quantizer for the same \(\sigma^2=400\) Gaussian source. Table value: \(D=0.03454\).

  1. Scale normalized reproduction values by \(\sigma=20\).
  2. The lecture's symmetric reconstruction levels are approximately \(\pm43.04,\ \pm26.88,\ \pm15.12,\ \pm4.902\).
  3. The corresponding boundaries are approximately \(\pm34.96,\ \pm21.00,\ \pm10.012,\ 0\).
  4. Scale distortion:
\[ D = 400(0.03454)=13.816 \]

Interpretation: Nonuniform quantization does better because it spends more levels where the Gaussian source is more likely to occur.

Example 6: PCM SQNR for a uniform source

Problem: A signal is uniformly distributed on \([-1,1]\). Uniform PCM uses 256 levels. Find the resulting SQNR.

  1. Signal power for uniform \([-1,1]\) is:
\[ P_X=\int_{-1}^{1}x^2\frac{1}{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{3} \]
  1. \(N=256=2^8\), so \(v=8\).
  2. Use \(SQNR = 3\cdot4^vP_X/x_{max}^2\), with \(x_{max}=1\):
\[ SQNR=3(4^8)\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)=4^8=65536 \]
\[ SQNR_{dB}=10\log_{10}(65536)\approx48.16\ \text{dB} \]

Example 7: Full PCM rate, bandwidth, and SQNR design

Problem: A zero-mean WSS process has triangular PSD over \(|f|\le5000\) Hz and maximum amplitude \(x_{max}=600\). Find power, sampling rate with 2 kHz guard band, SQNR and rate for 256 levels, transmission bandwidth, then increase SQNR by at least 25 dB.

  1. Power is the area under the triangular PSD: \(P_X=5000\) W.
  2. Sampling rate with guard band:
\[ f_s=2W+W_G=2(5000)+2000=12\ \text{kHz} \]
  1. For \(N=256\), \(v=8\). Compute SQNR:
\[ SQNR_{dB}=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{5000}{600^2}\right)+6(8)+4.8=34.2\ \text{dB} \]
  1. Bit rate and binary transmission bandwidth:
\[ R=vf_s=8(12\text{k})=96\ \text{kbps},\quad BW_T=\frac{R}{2}=48\ \text{kHz} \]
  1. At roughly 6 dB per bit, a 25 dB improvement needs 5 extra bits. Thus \(v=13\) and \(N=2^{13}=8192\).
  2. New SQNR and bandwidth:
\[ SQNR_{dB}=34.2+6(5)=64.2\ \text{dB},\quad BW_T=\frac{13(12\text{k})}{2}=78\ \text{kHz} \]

Section 5

Misconceptions & Exam Traps

Time mistake

Using \(W\) where \(f_s\) belongs, or forgetting \(T_s=1/f_s\).

Level mistake

Confusing \(N\) levels with \(v\) bits per sample.

Noise mistake

Using PCM SQNR when the problem asks for exact quantizer distortion.

Step mistake

For DM, large \(\Delta\) and small \(\Delta\) fail in different ways.

Trap 1: Confusing \(W\), \(f_s\), and \(T_s\)

\(W\) is signal bandwidth. \(f_s\) is samples per second. \(T_s=1/f_s\) is the time between samples. The Nyquist condition is \(f_s\ge2W\), equivalently \(T_s\le1/(2W)\).

Trap 2: Thinking linear interpolation is exact

The lecture shows linear interpolation as an intuitive approximation. Exact reconstruction under the sampling theorem uses ideal sinc interpolation or an ideal lowpass reconstruction filter.

Trap 3: Forgetting the guard band

If a problem says "guard band," do not answer only \(2W\). Use \(f_s = 2W + W_G\) when the guard band is specified as an added frequency gap.

Trap 4: Mixing up quantization levels and bits

\(N\) is the number of levels. \(v\) is bits per sample. They are related by \(N=2^v\). For 256 levels, \(v=8\), not 256 bits.

Trap 5: Using one SQNR formula blindly

The simple uniform PCM SQNR formula assumes many levels and approximately uniform quantization error. For fixed nonuniform regions or Gaussian-optimal quantizers, use the distortion integral or table-scaled distortion.

Trap 6: Choosing delta modulation step size by one criterion only

Large \(\Delta\) helps track steep slopes but causes granular noise. Small \(\Delta\) reduces granular noise but can create slope overload. ADM exists because the best step size changes with the signal.

Section 7

Key Terms Glossary

A-law compander A logarithmic speech companding curve commonly used to emphasize small amplitudes before uniform quantization.
ADM Adaptive Delta Modulation; delta modulation with a step size that changes according to recent slope behavior.
Aliasing Distortion caused when sampled spectrum replicas overlap, making high-frequency content appear as lower-frequency content.
Bandlimited signal A signal whose frequency-domain content is zero outside a finite bandwidth \(W\).
Bit rate Number of transmitted bits per second, \(R=vf_s\) for PCM.
Companding Compressing amplitudes before quantization and expanding them after decoding to approximate nonuniform quantization.
DPCM Differential PCM; encodes a prediction error between samples instead of encoding each sample directly.
Delta modulation One-bit DPCM-like scheme that tracks the signal using up/down steps of size \(\Delta\).
Distortion Average squared difference between source value and quantized value.
Encoding Assigning binary words to quantization levels.
Granular noise Delta modulation error caused by too large a step size on slowly changing signal regions.
Gray code Binary code in which adjacent quantization levels differ by one bit, reducing the impact of single-bit errors.
Guard band Extra frequency spacing that lets practical filters transition between passband and stopband.
Lloyd-Max conditions The centroid and midpoint conditions for optimal mean squared error scalar quantization.
Natural binary code Binary assignment where increasing binary words correspond to increasing quantizer levels.
Nyquist rate Minimum ideal sampling frequency \(2W\) for a lowpass bandlimited signal.
PCM Pulse Code Modulation; waveform encoding using sampler, quantizer, and encoder.
Quantization Mapping continuous amplitude samples to a finite set of allowed values.
Quantization region A range of input values mapped to the same reproduction level.
Reconstruction filter Lowpass filter used to recover the original signal from sampled spectrum replicas.
Sampling Measuring an analog signal at regularly spaced time instants.
Scalar quantization Quantizing each sample independently.
Sinc interpolation Ideal interpolation method for reconstructing a bandlimited signal from samples.
Slope overload Delta modulation error caused by too small a step size on rapidly changing signal regions.
SQNR Signal-to-quantization-noise ratio, \(P_X/D\).
Uniform quantizer Quantizer with equal interval widths except possibly the two outer overload regions.
Vector quantization Quantizing blocks of samples jointly rather than one sample at a time.
WSS process Wide-sense stationary random process; mean is constant and autocorrelation depends only on time difference.

Section 8

Summary & Revision Notes

Main idea

Think of ADC as a chain of design trades. Sampling protects time information. Quantization controls amplitude error. Encoding turns each chosen level into bits. PCM then asks whether the channel bandwidth can carry those bits.

When an exam question feels long, reduce it to this chain: \(W\rightarrow f_s\rightarrow N\rightarrow v\rightarrow R\rightarrow BW_T\rightarrow SQNR\).

Sampling: \(f_s\ge2W\), or \(f_s=2W+W_G\)
Levels to bits: \(N=2^v\)
Bit rate: \(R=vf_s\)
Uniform PCM: \(SQNR_{dB}\approx10\log_{10}(P_X/x_{max}^2)+6v+4.8\)
1

Time axis

Sampling turns \(x(t)\) into \(x(nT_s)\). Too slow means spectrum replicas overlap and aliasing appears.

2

Amplitude axis

Quantization rounds each sample to \(\hat{x}_i\). More levels reduce \(D\), but cost more bits.

3

Bit stream

Encoding maps \(N\) levels into \(v=\log_2N\) bits per sample, so \(R=vf_s\).

4

Waveform coding

PCM encodes samples directly. DPCM and DM exploit sample-to-sample correlation to reduce the data burden.

Revision checklist

  • Given bandwidth: compute \(f_s\) before touching bit rate.
  • Given levels: convert \(N\) to \(v\) immediately.
  • Given PSD: find power by area or integration.
  • Given quantizer regions: use the full distortion integral.
  • Given a required SQNR increase: count bits at roughly 6 dB per added bit.
  • Given delta modulation wording: compare granular noise against slope overload.

Practice questions

  1. \(W=6000\) Hz: find \(f_s\) with no guard band and with a 2000 Hz guard band. For the slide filter with passband to 7000 Hz and stopband after 10000 Hz, find the minimum \(f_s\) and \(K\).
    Show solution

    No guard band: \(f_s=2W=2(6000)=12000\) Hz.

    With a 2000 Hz guard band: \(f_s=2W+W_G=12000+2000=14000\) Hz.

    For the given reconstruction filter, the first replica starts at \(f_s-W\). To be fully inside the stopband, require \(f_s-W\ge10000\), so \(f_s\ge16000\) Hz. Because sampled spectra are scaled by \(1/T_s\), the reconstruction gain is \(K=T_s=1/f_s=1/16000\).

  2. \(P_X=10\): compare 16-level uniform and nonuniform Gaussian quantizers using the lecture tables.
    Show solution

    For a zero-mean Gaussian source, \(P_X=\sigma^2=10\), so \(\sigma=\sqrt{10}\).

    Uniform, \(N=16\): from Table 7.1, \(\Delta=0.3352\) and \(D_{table}=0.01154\). Scale distortion by variance: \(D=10(0.01154)=0.1154\).

    Nonuniform, \(N=16\): from Table 7.2, \(D_{table}=0.009497\). Thus \(D=10(0.009497)=0.09497\).

    Nonuniform is better because \(0.09497<0.1154\). In SQNR terms, uniform gives \(10\log_{10}(10/0.1154)=19.38\) dB, while nonuniform gives \(10\log_{10}(10/0.09497)=20.22\) dB.

  3. PCM design: use \(P_X=5000\), \(x_{max}=600\), \(W=5000\) Hz, \(W_G=2000\) Hz, and \(N=256\). Find \(R\), \(BW_T\), and \(SQNR_{dB}\). Then increase SQNR by at least 25 dB.
    Show solution

    Sampling rate: \(f_s=2W+W_G=2(5000)+2000=12000\) Hz.

    For \(N=256=2^8\), \(v=8\). Bit rate: \(R=vf_s=8(12000)=96000\) bps. Binary transmission bandwidth: \(BW_T=R/2=48000\) Hz.

    \(SQNR_{dB}=10\log_{10}(5000/600^2)+6(8)+4.8=34.2\) dB.

    At about 6 dB per extra bit, at least 25 dB needs 5 extra bits. So \(v=13\), \(N=2^{13}=8192\), \(SQNR_{dB}=34.2+30=64.2\) dB, \(R=13(12000)=156000\) bps, and \(BW_T=78000\) Hz.

  4. Delta modulation: explain what happens when \(\Delta\) is too large and when \(\Delta\) is too small.
    Show solution

    Large \(\Delta\): the staircase can follow steep slopes, but during slow signal changes it jumps too much around the true waveform. That creates granular noise.

    Small \(\Delta\): the staircase is smooth during gentle regions, but it cannot climb or fall fast enough when the input slope is large. That creates slope overload distortion.

    Adaptive delta modulation reduces this tradeoff by increasing \(\Delta\) after repeated same-sign steps and decreasing \(\Delta\) after alternating signs.

  5. WSS Gaussian PCM: \(R_X(\tau)=\operatorname{sinc}^2(10^4\tau)\), \(x_{max}=10\), \(N=128\), and guard band \(W_G=2.5\) kHz. Find \(f_s\), bit rate, SQNR, and transmission bandwidth.
    Show solution

    \(\operatorname{sinc}^2(10^4\tau)\) transforms to a triangular spectrum with support \(|f|\le10^4\) Hz, so \(W=10\) kHz. Also \(P_X=R_X(0)=1\).

    With \(W_G=2.5\) kHz, \(f_s=2W+W_G=20+2.5=22.5\) kHz.

    \(N=128=2^7\), so \(v=7\). Bit rate: \(R=7(22.5\text{ kHz})=157.5\) kbps. Transmission bandwidth: \(BW_T=R/2=78.75\) kHz.

    \(SQNR_{dB}=10\log_{10}(1/10^2)+6(7)+4.8=-20+42+4.8=26.8\) dB.

  6. A sinusoid at \(f_0=7\) kHz is sampled at \(f_s=10\) kHz. Is this alias-free? If not, what alias frequency appears? What is the minimum sampling frequency to avoid aliasing?
    Show solution

    For a sinusoid at \(f_0=7\) kHz, the Nyquist condition requires \(f_s\ge2f_0=14\) kHz. Since \(10\) kHz is below \(14\) kHz, it is not alias-free.

    The apparent alias frequency is the distance to the nearest integer multiple of \(f_s\):

    \[ f_{alias}=|f_0-1f_s|=|7-10|=3\text{ kHz} \]

    So the sampled sinusoid can look like a \(3\) kHz sinusoid. Minimum alias-free sampling is \(14\) kHz.

  7. A uniform midrise quantizer covers \([-2,2]\) with \(N=16\) levels. Find \(\Delta\), bits per sample, \(Q(0.70)\), the quantization error, and the approximate quantization-noise power.
    Show solution

    The total range length is \(4\), so:

    \[ \Delta=\frac{4}{16}=0.25,\qquad v=\log_2(16)=4\text{ bits/sample} \]

    For a midrise quantizer, the level in the interval \([0.50,0.75)\) is the midpoint \(0.625\). Since \(0.70\in[0.50,0.75)\), \(Q(0.70)=0.625\).

    \[ e=x-Q(x)=0.70-0.625=0.075 \]

    Approximate quantization-noise power:

    \[ D\approx\frac{\Delta^2}{12}=\frac{0.25^2}{12}=0.00521 \]
  8. A uniform PCM system has \(P_X=0.25\) and \(x_{max}=1\). How many bits per sample are needed for \(SQNR_{dB}\ge40\) dB? How many levels is that?
    Show solution

    Use the uniform PCM approximation:

    \[ SQNR_{dB}\approx10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_X}{x_{max}^2}\right)+6v+4.8 \]

    Substitute \(P_X=0.25\) and \(x_{max}=1\):

    \[ 10\log_{10}(0.25)+6v+4.8=-6.02+6v+4.8=6v-1.22 \]

    Require \(6v-1.22\ge40\), so \(v\ge6.87\). Since \(v\) must be an integer, choose \(v=7\).

    \[ N=2^7=128,\qquad SQNR_{dB}\approx6(7)-1.22=40.78\text{ dB} \]
  9. A PCM system directly quantizes samples over \([-4,4]\). A DPCM system quantizes only prediction errors over \([-0.5,0.5]\). If both use uniform step size \(\Delta=0.25\), compare the bits per sample needed.
    Show solution

    For direct PCM, the range length is \(8\):

    \[ N_{PCM}=\frac{8}{0.25}=32,\qquad v_{PCM}=\log_2(32)=5 \]

    For DPCM prediction errors, the range length is \(1\):

    \[ N_{DPCM}=\frac{1}{0.25}=4,\qquad v_{DPCM}=\log_2(4)=2 \]

    DPCM saves \(5-2=3\) bits per sample in this setup, because it quantizes a much smaller dynamic range.

  10. For a normalized positive sample \(x=0.10\), calculate the \(\mu\)-law compressor output using \(\mu=255\). What does the result tell you?
    Show solution

    For \(x>0\), \(\operatorname{sgn}(x)=1\), so:

    \[ g_\mu(x)=\frac{\log(1+\mu x)}{\log(1+\mu)} =\frac{\log(1+255(0.10))}{\log(256)} =\frac{\log(26.5)}{\log(256)} \]
    \[ g_\mu(0.10)\approx\frac{3.277}{5.545}=0.591 \]

    A small input amplitude, \(0.10\), is mapped to a much larger compressed-domain value, about \(0.591\). This gives small speech amplitudes more effective quantization resolution.

  11. In adaptive delta modulation, let \(K=1.5\), \(\Delta_0=0.10\), and quantizer signs \(\epsilon_0=+1,\epsilon_1=+1,\epsilon_2=-1,\epsilon_3=-1,\epsilon_4=+1\). Use \(\Delta_n=\Delta_{n-1}K^{\epsilon_n\epsilon_{n-1}}\) to find \(\Delta_1\) to \(\Delta_4\).
    Show solution

    Same consecutive signs increase the step size. Opposite consecutive signs decrease it.

    \[ \Delta_1=\Delta_0K^{(+1)(+1)}=0.10(1.5)=0.15 \]
    \[ \Delta_2=\Delta_1K^{(-1)(+1)}=0.15/1.5=0.10 \]
    \[ \Delta_3=\Delta_2K^{(-1)(-1)}=0.10(1.5)=0.15 \]
    \[ \Delta_4=\Delta_3K^{(+1)(-1)}=0.15/1.5=0.10 \]
