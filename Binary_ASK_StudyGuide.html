Binary Amplitude-Shift Keying (BASK)

The complete guide to baseband rectangular pulses and carrier modulation energy

Chapter 1: The Physical Intuition

What is Binary ASK?

1. The Problem

In baseband digital communication, we transmit bits using simple rectangular voltage pulses.

However, antennas cannot effectively transmit baseband (DC) signals over the air.

2. The Solution

We modulate a high-frequency sine wave (the carrier wave, $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$).

We use our baseband pulse $p(t)$ to strictly dictate the amplitude of this carrier wave.

How we assign bits:

  • Bit 1 ➔ Transmit positive amplitude: $s_1(t) = \;\;p(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)$
  • Bit 0 ➔ Transmit negative amplitude: $s_2(t) = -p(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)$

Notice that we are just multiplying a high-frequency sine wave by a rectangular envelope!

Chapter 2: The Core Derivation

Deriving the Amplitude of $p(t)$

Your lecture slide states a very specific, magical-looking formula for the rectangular pulse $p(t)$:

$$ p(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}} $$

Where does this come from? Let's derive it from first principles. Our goal is to find the amplitude $A$ of the rectangular pulse $p(t)$ such that the total energy of the modulated carrier signal $s_1(t)$ equals exactly $\mathcal{E}_b$.

Step 1 The Energy Formula

We know the energy of any signal is the integral of its square. Let's substitute $s_1(t) = A \cos(2\pi f_c t)$ into the equation:

$$ \mathcal{E}_b = \int_{0}^{T_b} (s_1(t))^2 dt = \int_{0}^{T_b} \left( A \cos(2\pi f_c t) \right)^2 dt $$

Step 2 The Trigonometric Identity

Squaring a cosine is difficult to integrate directly. We use the double-angle power-reduction identity: $\cos^2(\theta) = \frac{1 + \cos(2\theta)}{2}$.

$$ \mathcal{E}_b = \int_{0}^{T_b} A^2 \left( \frac{1 + \cos(4\pi f_c t)}{2} \right) dt $$

Let's pull the constants out front and split the integral into two separate parts for clarity:

$$ \mathcal{E}_b = \frac{A^2}{2} \left[ \int_{0}^{T_b} 1 dt \quad + \quad \int_{0}^{T_b} \cos(4\pi f_c t) dt \right] $$

Step 3 The High-Frequency Trick

Look at the second integral term:

$$ \int_{0}^{T_b} \cos(4\pi f_c t) dt \approx 0 $$

Why is this exactly zero?

  • The carrier frequency $f_c$ is extremely high (e.g., Megahertz or Gigahertz).
  • This means there are millions of full cosine cycles squeezed inside a single bit duration $T_b$.
  • Because the area under the positive half-cycles perfectly cancels the area under the negative half-cycles, integrating over full cycles yields zero!

Step 4 Solving for Amplitude $A$

Now, our massive energy equation simplifies down to something incredibly clean:

$$ \mathcal{E}_b = \frac{A^2}{2} \left[ \int_{0}^{T_b} 1 dt \right] = \frac{A^2}{2} \Big[ t \Big]_0^{T_b} = \frac{A^2 \cdot T_b}{2} $$

We just algebraically solve for $A$ (the amplitude of $p(t)$):

$$ A^2 = \frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b} $$
$$ p(t) = A = \sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}} $$
The Real Conclusion:
The factor of $\sqrt{2}$ appears because squaring a high-frequency sine wave averages out to $\frac{1}{2}$ its peak power. To guarantee we transmit exactly $\mathcal{E}_b$ joules of energy, we must mathematically pump the amplitude up by $\sqrt{2}$ to compensate!

Wait, what about $s_2(t)$ and the Unit Energy Equation $\psi(t)$?

1. Why do $s_1(t)$ and $s_2(t)$ both have energy $\mathcal{E}_b$?

In Binary Antipodal Signaling, the bit 0 signal is the exact negative of the bit 1 signal: $s_2(t) = -s_1(t)$.

Because energy is calculated by squaring the signal, the negative sign disappears completely:

$$ \text{Energy of } s_2(t) = \int_{0}^{T_b} (-s_1(t))^2 dt = \int_{0}^{T_b} (s_1(t))^2 dt = \mathcal{E}_b $$

Both signals transmit the exact same amount of physical energy!

2. Where does the Unit Energy Equation $\psi(t)$ come from?

In the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization process, we need a normalized basis function $\psi(t)$ that has exactly $1 \text{ Joule}$ of energy. We get this by simply dividing our signal $s_1(t)$ by the square root of its own energy $\mathcal{E}_b$:

$$ \psi(t) = \frac{s_1(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}} $$

Now, let's substitute $s_1(t) = p(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)$ and $p(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}}$ into the equation:

$$ \psi(t) = \frac{\sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}} \cos(2\pi f_c t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}} $$

The $\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}$ terms mathematically cancel out, leaving us with the beautiful, pure basis function from Slide 18:

$$ \psi(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{T_b}} \cos(2\pi f_c t) $$

Chapter 3: Worked Numerical Examples

Example 1: Finding the Required Pulse Amplitude

Problem: A wireless BASK system operates at a bit rate of $1 \text{ Mbps}$. The system requires an energy per bit of $\mathcal{E}_b = 4 \times 10^{-6} \text{ Joules}$ to overcome noise. What is the required amplitude $A$ of the baseband rectangular pulse $p(t)$?

Solution Walkthrough:

Step 1 Find the bit duration $T_b$. The bit rate $R_b = 10^6 \text{ bits/sec}$, so:

$$ T_b = \frac{1}{R_b} = \frac{1}{10^6} = 10^{-6} \text{ seconds (1 } \mu\text{s)} $$

Step 2 Use the derived amplitude formula for the rectangular pulse:

$$ A = \sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times (4 \times 10^{-6})}{10^{-6}}} $$

Step 3 Solve the math:

$$ A = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.828 \text{ Volts} $$
Final Answer: The baseband pulse must have an amplitude of $2.828 \text{ V}$ to deliver the required energy.

Example 2: Determining Transmitted Energy

Problem: A BASK transmitter uses a rectangular pulse $p(t)$ with a fixed amplitude of $10 \text{ V}$. If the bit duration is $2 \text{ ms}$, how much energy is contained in a single transmitted '1' bit?

Solution Walkthrough:

Step 1 Rearrange the formula to solve for energy $\mathcal{E}_b$. We know $A = 10$ and $T_b = 0.002$.

$$ \mathcal{E}_b = \frac{A^2 \cdot T_b}{2} $$

Step 2 Plug in the values:

$$ \mathcal{E}_b = \frac{(10)^2 \cdot 0.002}{2} = \frac{100 \cdot 0.002}{2} $$

Step 3 Calculate the final energy:

$$ \mathcal{E}_b = \frac{0.2}{2} = 0.1 \text{ Joules} $$
Final Answer: Each bit contains $0.1 \text{ J}$ of energy.

Chapter 4: Misconceptions & Exam Traps

⚠️ Common Pitfalls to Avoid

Trap 1: Forgetting the factor of 2! If asked for the energy of a baseband rectangular pulse alone, it is $A^2 T_b$. However, if that pulse is modulating a passband carrier wave, the energy is $\frac{A^2 T_b}{2}$. The carrier wave oscillates, meaning it spends half its time at lower voltages, thus delivering exactly half the power of a solid DC wave.
Trap 2: Assuming $s_2(t)$ has zero energy in BASK. In binary orthogonal signaling (like On-Off Keying), bit 0 might have 0 energy. But in Slide 18, $s_2(t)$ is defined as $-p(t)\cos(2\pi f_c t)$. Squaring this negative sign mathematically yields positive energy. Both $s_1(t)$ and $s_2(t)$ transmit exactly $\mathcal{E}_b$ joules! This is specifically Antipodal ASK (also identical to BPSK).
Trap 3: Integrating the cosine. Do not waste time manually integrating $\cos(4\pi f_c t)$ during an exam. State explicitly: "Assuming $f_c \gg \frac{1}{T_b}$, the integral of the double-frequency term over the bit period evaluates to zero."

Key Terms Glossary

Baseband Signal A signal whose frequencies start at zero (DC). The rectangular envelope pulse $p(t)$ is a baseband signal.
Passband Signal A high-frequency signal centered around a carrier frequency $f_c$. The resulting modulated wave $s(t)$ is passband.
Carrier Frequency ($f_c$) The frequency of the unmodulated sine wave used to carry the data across the physical channel (e.g., air, fiber).
Gram-Schmidt A mathematical process used to find the normalized basis functions $\psi(t)$ of a set of signals.

📝 Summary & Revision Notes

  • 1. To transmit digital bits over the air, we multiply a baseband pulse by a high-frequency sine wave.
  • 2. Because squaring a sine wave averages out to $1/2$, a passband signal only delivers half the power of an equivalent DC signal.
  • 3. To compensate for this power loss and deliver a target energy $\mathcal{E}_b$, we must scale the amplitude of our baseband pulse $p(t)$ by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$.
  • 4. This mathematically results in the famous formula: $p(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}}$.
  • 5. If you use Antipodal BASK ($s_2 = -s_1$), both the '1' and '0' bits carry identical energy $\mathcal{E}_b$.
