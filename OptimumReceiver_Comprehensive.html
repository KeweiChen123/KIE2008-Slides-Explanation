Optimum Receiver & Error Probability

The Sudden Shift to Hardware

After 27 slides of abstract vector math, the slides suddenly throw you into hardware architecture. Why? Because we need to figure out how to physically catch the signal out of thin air, clean off the noise, and guess what was sent.

1. The AWGN Channel (The Real World)

Signals don't survive the real world untouched.

When your beautiful signal $s_m(t)$ flies through the air, heat, cosmic radiation, and electronics bombard it with static. We model this as an AWGN channel.

  • A (Additive): The noise physically stacks on top of our signal.
  • W (White): It has energy at every single frequency (like white light).
  • G (Gaussian): The noise amplitude follows a Bell Curve probability.
  • N (Noise): It's random static!
The Channel Math:
$$ r(t) = s_m(t) + n(t) $$
  • $r(t)$: What your antenna catches (The Mess)
  • $s_m(t)$: What was transmitted (The Truth)
  • $n(t)$: The random AWGN static (The Noise)
The Noise Power:
$$ S_n(f) = \frac{N_0}{2} \text{ (W/Hz)} $$

Because "White" noise is everywhere, its power spectral density is flat. $N_0/2$ is just a constant value representing how loud the background static is.

2. The Optimum Receiver Architecture

How do we process the dirty signal $r(t)$?

We build an Optimum Receiver designed to minimize the Probability of Error ($P_E$). Slide 28 splits this receiver into two highly distinct hardware blocks:

1. The Demodulator

Role: The Translator
  • No Decisions: The Demodulator does NOT guess the signal.
  • Math Translation: It literally strips away the high-frequency radio wave.
  • Output: It cleanly converts the messy wave $r(t)$ into a Math Vector ($\mathbf{y}$) using the basis functions $\psi(t)$.
$$ r(t) \xrightarrow{\text{Demodulate}} \mathbf{y} = [y_1, y_2, \dots, y_N] $$

2. The Detector

Role: The Judge
  • The Input: It strictly looks at the newly translated vector $\mathbf{y}$.
  • The Problem: Because of noise, $\mathbf{y}$ won't land perfectly on $s_1$ or $s_2$. It lands randomly in between them.
  • The Decision: It calculates Euclidean Distance to find the closest valid signal point, minimizing $P_E$.
$$ \text{Is } \mathbf{y} \text{ closer to } \mathbf{s_1} \text{ or } \mathbf{s_2}? \xrightarrow{\text{Judge}} \hat{m} $$

4. Demodulator Types (Slide 29)

Now that we know what the Demodulator does, how does it physically do it? Slide 29 introduces two completely different hardware approaches for building a Demodulator.

Type A: Correlation-Type

Coherent Demodulation
  • The Method
    The received signal is multiplied by a complex reference signal.
  • Phase Sync Required
    The complex reference signal is generated by mixing a local oscillator with the transmitted carrier signal and is synchronized in phase with the received carrier signal.
  • Low-Pass Filter
    The complex signal is then low-pass filtered to remove high-frequency components, leaving only the pure baseband signal.
  • Pros
    Offers high accuracy and excellent noise immunity.
  • Cons
    Highly complex and expensive to implement.

Type B: Matched-Filter

Non-Coherent Demodulation
  • The Method
    The received signal is filtered by a matched filter designed to strictly match the characteristics of the transmitted signal.
  • No Phase Sync
    It is a non-coherent technique (completely ignores the carrier phase).
  • Sampling
    The output of the matched filter is then sampled at the appropriate rate to successfully recover the transmitted digital data.
  • Pros
    It is simple and low-cost to implement physically.
  • Cons
    More prone to noise interference compared to coherent detection.
5. Calculating Probability of Error (Slide 30)

The Detector makes a guess, but what are the chances it guesses wrong? The Probability of Error ($P_E$) depends heavily on the Euclidean distance between the signal points. The further apart they are, the harder it is for noise to push one signal into the other's territory.

The Master $P_E$ Formula (Euclidean Distance):
$$ P_E = Q \left( \sqrt{ \frac{d_{12}^2}{2N_0} } \right) $$
  • $Q(\cdot)$: The Q-function (the tail probability of the Gaussian noise curve).
  • $d_{12}$: The Euclidean distance between signal $\mathbf{s_1}$ and $\mathbf{s_2}$. Bigger distance = Lower $P_E$!
  • $N_0$: The noise power density. More noise = Higher $P_E$!

Binary Antipodal Signals

(e.g., $s_1(t)$ and $s_2(t) = -s_1(t)$)

$$ P_E = Q \left( \sqrt{ \frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{N_0} } \right) $$
The Mathematical Proof (Exam Prep):

Substitute $s_2(t) = -s_1(t)$ into $d_{12}^2$:

\begin{align*} d_{12}^2 &= \int_0^{T_b} [s_1(t) - (-s_1(t))]^2 dt \\ &= \int_0^{T_b} [2s_1(t)]^2 dt \\ &= 4 \int_0^{T_b} s_1^2(t) dt \\ &= \mathbf{4\mathcal{E}_b} \end{align*}

Binary Orthogonal Signals

(e.g., $\int s_1(t)s_2(t) dt = 0$)

$$ P_E = Q \left( \sqrt{ \frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{N_0} } \right) $$
The Mathematical Proof (Exam Prep):

Expand the square and apply orthogonality ($\int s_1 s_2 dt = 0$):

\begin{align*} d_{12}^2 &= \int_0^{T_b} [s_1(t) - s_2(t)]^2 dt \\ &= \int_0^{T_b} [s_1^2(t) - 2s_1(t)s_2(t) + s_2^2(t)] dt \\ &= \int_0^{T_b} s_1^2(t) dt - 2(0) + \int_0^{T_b} s_2^2(t) dt \\ &= \mathcal{E}_b + \mathcal{E}_b = \mathbf{2\mathcal{E}_b} \end{align*}
🔥 The 3dB Advantage (Exam Tip!):

Look closely at the numerator inside the square roots. Antipodal has $2\mathcal{E}_b$, while Orthogonal only has $\mathcal{E}_b$. This means Antipodal signals require exactly HALF the signal energy (a 3dB power saving) to achieve the exact same probability of error as Orthogonal signals! This is because antipodal points are spread much further apart across the origin.

6. Worked Example: Bit Error Probability (Slide 31)

This is a classic university exam question. You are given two piecewise signal waveforms and must calculate the exact Bit Error Probability from scratch using calculus.

The Given Signals:

$s_1(t)$ is a step function:

$$ s_1(t) = \begin{cases} 2 & 0 \leq t < \frac{T_b}{2} \\ 1 & \frac{T_b}{2} \leq t < T_b \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$

$s_2(t)$ is a linear ramp:

$$ s_2(t) = \begin{cases} \frac{2t}{T_b} & 0 \leq t < T_b \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$
The Calculus Derivation:

To find $P_E$, we first need to evaluate the distance integral $d_{12}^2$. Because $s_1(t)$ changes value halfway through, we must split the integral into two pieces:

\begin{align*} d_{12}^2 &= \int_0^{T_b} [s_1(t) - s_2(t)]^2 dt \\\\ &= \int_0^{T_b/2} \left[2 - \frac{2t}{T_b}\right]^2 dt + \int_{T_b/2}^{T_b} \left[1 - \frac{2t}{T_b}\right]^2 dt \\\\ &= \int_0^{T_b/2} \left(4 - \frac{8t}{T_b} + \frac{4t^2}{T_b^2}\right) dt + \int_{T_b/2}^{T_b} \left(1 - \frac{4t}{T_b} + \frac{4t^2}{T_b^2}\right) dt \end{align*}

Now, carry out the integration and plug in the limits step-by-step:

\begin{align*} &= \left[ 4t - \frac{4t^2}{T_b} + \frac{4t^3}{3T_b^2} \right]_0^{T_b/2} + \left[ t - \frac{2t^2}{T_b} + \frac{4t^3}{3T_b^2} \right]_{T_b/2}^{T_b} \\\\ &= \left( 2T_b - T_b + \frac{T_b}{6} \right) + \left( \left[ T_b - 2T_b + \frac{4}{3}T_b \right] - \left[ \frac{T_b}{2} - \frac{T_b}{2} + \frac{T_b}{6} \right] \right) \\\\ &= \left( \frac{7}{6}T_b \right) + \left( \frac{1}{3}T_b - \frac{1}{6}T_b \right) \\\\ &= \frac{7}{6}T_b + \frac{1}{6}T_b = \frac{8}{6}T_b \end{align*}

The heavy algebra collapses beautifully to our final answer:

\begin{align*} d_{12}^2 &= \frac{4}{3}T_b \\\\ \implies P_E &= Q \left( \sqrt{ \frac{d_{12}^2}{2N_0} } \right) = \mathbf{Q \left( \sqrt{ \frac{2T_b}{3N_0} } \right)} \end{align*}
