Chapter 1: The Bridge – From Math to Modulation

💡 The "Why" behind Gram-Schmidt

You just spent hours learning the Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization Procedure. But why? How does finding orthogonal vectors have anything to do with transmitting bits across a cable or through the air?

Here is the reality: Cables and antennas don't understand abstract 1s and 0s. They only understand physical, continuous voltage or electromagnetic waves over time (like $s_1(t)$ and $s_2(t)$).

The choice of what $s_1(t)$ and $s_2(t)$ actually look like is called Modulation. For example, if you want to send a '1', you send a positive voltage pulse. To send a '0', you send a negative voltage pulse. That's modulation in the physical world!

So where does Gram-Schmidt fit in?

Imagine trying to calculate how much a physical signal degrades when random noise (static) is added to it in a wire. Trying to do math on squiggly, noisy time-domain waveforms $s(t)$ is incredibly difficult and messy.

Gram-Schmidt is the map. It allows us to take those complex physical waveforms and plot them as simple geometric points (vectors) on a graph called a Signal Space.

  • Modulation is picking the physical waves to send.
  • Gram-Schmidt creates the $X$ and $Y$ axes (the basis functions $\psi_1, \psi_2$) so we can map those waves into dots on a screen.

Chapter 2: What is Binary Antipodal Signaling?

There are many ways to choose $s_1(t)$ and $s_2(t)$. One of the most famous and efficient methods is called Binary Antipodal Signaling.

  • "Binary" = We are dealing with exactly $M = 2$ signals (to send 1 bit per symbol).
  • "Antipodal" = The two waveforms are exact geometric opposites of each other (like the North and South poles).
Definition: For a given base pulse $s(t)$, we define:
Bit '1': $s_1(t) = s(t)$
Bit '0': $s_2(t) = -s(t)$

Why is this the same as PAM?

Your lecturer mentioned that this is the same as Pulse Amplitude Modulation (PAM). Why? Because we are taking a basic pulse shape $p(t)$ and modulating its amplitude.

If we set our amplitude to either $+A$ or $-A$, our signals become:

  • $s_1(t) = +A \cdot p(t)$
  • $s_2(t) = -A \cdot p(t)$

Notice that $s_2(t)$ is just exactly $-s_1(t)$. We are just flipping the signal upside down. Because we are only changing the amplitude of a pulse, this is fundamentally a 2-level PAM system (also known as 2-PAM or BPSK if the pulse is a sine wave).

The Energy of Antipodal Signals

Let's use the most straightforward mathematical approach to prove that both bits have the exact same energy. The energy of any signal is simply the integral of its square.

We defined $s_1(t) = s(t)$ and $s_2(t) = -s(t)$. Let's calculate the energy of each:

$$ \mathcal{E}_1 = \int_0^{T_b} (s_1(t))^2 dt = \int_0^{T_b} (s(t))^2 dt = \mathcal{E}_b $$
$$ \mathcal{E}_2 = \int_0^{T_b} (s_2(t))^2 dt = \int_0^{T_b} (-s(t))^2 dt $$

When we square $-s(t)$, the negative sign mathematically vanishes: $(-s(t))^2 = (s(t))^2$. Therefore:

$$ \mathcal{E}_2 = \int_0^{T_b} (s(t))^2 dt = \mathcal{E}_b $$

Conclusion: $\mathcal{E}_1 = \mathcal{E}_2 = \mathcal{E}_b$. In Binary Antipodal Signaling, transmitting a '0' costs the exact same amount of energy as transmitting a '1'!

What is $g_T(t)$ and how does it relate to $T_b$?

You will often see the base pulse $p(t)$ or $s(t)$ written as $g_T(t)$ in textbooks. This stands for the Transmitter Pulse Shape. It is the physical electrical waveform used to carry a bit.

$T_b$ (Bit Duration) is the physical amount of time allocated to transmit a single bit (e.g., if you transmit 1000 bits per second, $T_b = 1/1000 = 1$ ms).

The Relationship

  • Timing: In a basic system, the pulse $g_T(t)$ is emitted once every $T_b$ seconds. It typically turns "on" at $t=0$ and "off" at $t=T_b$.
  • Energy ($\mathcal{E}_b$): The energy of your transmitted bit is dictated by the shape of $g_T(t)$ and how long it lasts ($T_b$). We calculate it by integrating the squared pulse over the bit duration: $$\mathcal{E}_b = \int_0^{T_b} g_T^2(t) dt$$
  • Bandwidth: A shorter $T_b$ (faster internet) compresses $g_T(t)$ in time. Physics dictates that compressing a signal in time spreads it out in frequency, meaning you need more bandwidth!
🤔

The Energy Formula Confusion: $s(t)$ vs $g_T(t)$

You might be wondering: "Is the energy formula $\int s^2$? Or $\int A^2 g_T^2$? Or just $\int g_T^2$?"

The absolute, fundamental formula for Energy of ANY signal $x(t)$ in the universe is simply: $\int x^2(t) dt$

The confusion comes from how textbooks define the signals:

  1. If the entire physical waveform is called $s(t)$, then Energy = $\int s^2(t) dt$
  2. If the textbook splits the waveform into an amplitude $A$ and a basic shape $g_T(t)$ (so $s(t) = A \cdot g_T(t)$), then Energy = $\int (A \cdot g_T(t))^2 dt$ = $A^2 \int g_T^2(t) dt$
  3. If $g_T(t)$ is defined as the entire waveform (including its amplitude), then Energy = $\int g_T^2(t) dt$

Don't let the letters confuse you! Just square whatever the entire physical voltage is, and integrate it over time.

📝 Lecturer's Example 2: The Split-Phase (Manchester) Pulse

Your lecturer showed an example where the pulse changes sign halfway through the bit duration. The physical voltage (which they called $g_T(t)$) is defined as:

  • $g_T(t) = +\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}}$ for the first half: $0 \le t \le \frac{T_b}{2}$
  • $g_T(t) = -\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}}$ for the second half: $\frac{T_b}{2} \le t \le T_b$

When you square the negative second half, the minus sign disappears! Both amplitudes simply become $\frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}$:

$$ \text{Energy} = \int_{0}^{T_b/2} \frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b} dt + \int_{T_b/2}^{T_b} \frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b} dt $$

Now, evaluate the integrals by multiplying the constant squared-amplitude by the duration of each half (which is exactly $\frac{T_b}{2}$):

$$ \text{Energy} = \left[ \frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b} \times \frac{T_b}{2} \right] + \left[ \frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b} \times \frac{T_b}{2} \right] $$
$$ \text{Energy} = \frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{2} + \frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{2} = \mathcal{E}_b $$

The Takeaway: Even though the signal flips upside down halfway through, its total energy still perfectly equals $\mathcal{E}_b$ because squaring a negative voltage yields positive energy. This is a very common trick question in exams!

Finding the Basis Function $\psi_1(t)$

To find the geometric representation of this system, we must apply the first step of Gram-Schmidt: finding the basis function $\psi_1(t)$ by normalizing our signal $s_1(t)$ (which is $g_T(t)$).

$$ \psi_1(t) = \frac{s_1(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_1}} = \frac{g_T(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}} $$

Because we divide the signal by $\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}$, the amplitude of the pulse changes! We divide both halves by $\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}$:

  • First half: $+\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}} \div \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b} = \mathbf{+\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_b}}}$
  • Second half: $-\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}} \div \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b} = \mathbf{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_b}}}$

Notice that the shape of $\psi_1(t)$ is completely identical to $g_T(t)$! It is still a split-phase pulse. The only difference is the Y-axis amplitude, which now depends purely on time ($T_b$) and guarantees that $\psi_1(t)$ has exactly unit energy ($1$ Joule).

The Geometric Coordinates:

Chapter 3: The Proof – Why N = 1

The Core Question

If we have 2 signals ($M=2$), you might instinctively think we need a 2-dimensional space ($N=2$) with a $\psi_1(t)$ and a $\psi_2(t)$ to represent them. However, for Binary Antipodal Signaling, your lecturer stated that $N=1$. Let's prove exactly why $\psi_2(t)$ does not exist using Gram-Schmidt!

📐 Step-by-Step Gram-Schmidt Proof

We are given our two signals: $s_1(t)$ and $s_2(t) = -s_1(t)$.

Step 1: Find the first basis function $\psi_1(t)$

The energy of the first signal is $\mathcal{E}_1 = \int s_1^2(t) dt$. We normalize $s_1(t)$ to find our first axis:

$$\psi_1(t) = \frac{s_1(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_1}}$$

Step 2: Try to find the second basis function $\psi_2(t)$

To find the second axis, we must first find how much of $s_2(t)$ overlaps with $\psi_1(t)$. This is the projection coefficient $c_{21}$:

$$c_{21} = \int s_2(t)\psi_1(t) dt$$

Substitute $s_2(t) = -s_1(t)$ and $\psi_1(t) = \frac{s_1(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_1}}$:

$$c_{21} = \int (-s_1(t)) \left( \frac{s_1(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_1}} \right) dt = \frac{-1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_1}} \int s_1^2(t) dt$$

Since $\int s_1^2(t) dt = \mathcal{E}_1$, this simplifies to:

$$c_{21} = \frac{-\mathcal{E}_1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_1}} = -\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_1}$$

Now, we subtract this projection from $s_2(t)$ to find the remaining orthogonal piece $d_2(t)$:

$$d_2(t) = s_2(t) - c_{21}\psi_1(t)$$

Substitute $s_2(t) = -s_1(t)$ and $c_{21} = -\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_1}$:

$$d_2(t) = -s_1(t) - (-\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_1})\left( \frac{s_1(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_1}} \right)$$

$$d_2(t) = -s_1(t) - (-s_1(t))$$

$$d_2(t) = -s_1(t) + s_1(t) = 0$$

Conclusion: Because $d_2(t) = 0$, there is absolutely no signal energy left that is perpendicular to $\psi_1(t)$. We cannot normalize $0$ to create $\psi_2(t)$. Therefore, the process stops. The entire system can be perfectly represented using only ONE basis function ($N=1$).

Chapter 4: Geometric Representation & Signal Space

Since $N=1$, our signal space is no longer a 2D plane—it is a simple 1-dimensional number line. The axis is our basis function $\psi_1(t)$.

From our proof above, we can map our signals into vectors (coordinates):

  • For bit 1: $\mathbf{s}_1 = [+\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}]$
  • For bit 0: $\mathbf{s}_2 = [-\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}]$

(Note: We use $\mathcal{E}_b$ here to denote the average Energy per bit, since $\mathcal{E}_1 = \mathcal{E}_2 = \mathcal{E}_b$)

Interactive Visual ⚡: The 1D Signal Space

Look at the diagram below. Both signals sit on exactly the same axis, just on opposite sides of the origin. This represents maximum separation for a given amount of energy!

The Distance Metric ($d$):

The distance between the two signals dictates how easily noise can cause an error. A larger distance is better.

$$d_{12} = \sqrt{(s_{11} - s_{21})^2} = \sqrt{(\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b} - (-\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}))^2} = \sqrt{(2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b})^2} = 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}$$

Antipodal signaling is optimal because it maximizes this distance ($2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}$) for a given average signal energy. If they weren't antipodal, they would be closer together, making it easier for noise to confuse the receiver!

Chapter 5: Exam Traps & Misconceptions

🚨

Trap 1: "There are two signals ($s_1, s_2$), so it must be a 2D space ($N=2$)."

✅
The dimension $N$ is determined by the number of linearly independent signals, not just the total number of signals.

Because $s_2(t) = -s_1(t)$, it is just a scaled version (scaled by -1) of the first signal. It is completely linearly dependent. As we proved mathematically, the Gram-Schmidt process will yield $d_2(t) = 0$, meaning no $\psi_2$ is created. The space is strictly 1D.

🚨

Trap 2: "Antipodal Signaling requires sine waves (like BPSK)."

✅
The base pulse shape $s(t)$ can be absolutely anything!

Antipodal signaling is defined by the relationship $s_2(t) = -s_1(t)$, NOT by the shape of the pulse. The pulse could be a square wave, a triangle, a raised cosine pulse, or a high-frequency sine wave. As long as you are flipping that exact pulse upside down to send the other bit, it is antipodal signaling and it operates in a 1D signal space.

🚨

Trap 3: "The energy of $s_2(t)$ is negative because its amplitude is negative."

✅
Energy is ALWAYS positive. $\mathcal{E}_1 = \mathcal{E}_2 = \mathcal{E}_b$.

Energy is calculated by integrating the squared signal ($\int s^2(t) dt$). When you square a negative signal, $(-s_1(t))^2 = s_1^2(t)$. Therefore, the energy of a negative pulse is exactly the same as the energy of the positive pulse. In the signal space, both points are at a distance of exactly $\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}$ from the origin.
