M-Ary Digital Modulations: Beyond Binary

1. The Concept of Symbols

Previously, we only looked at sending one bit at a time using two waveforms (like Antipodal or Orthogonal signaling). But what if we want to transmit data much faster? We can transmit multiple bits simultaneously by grouping them together!

Bits vs Symbols

The binary information sequence is subdivided into chunks of $k$ bits. These chunks are called Symbols.

  • $k = 1$ (Binary)
    A symbol is just 1 bit. We only need $2^1 = 2$ unique physical waveforms.
  • $k = 2$ (e.g., 00, 01, 10, 11)
    A symbol is 2 bits. We need $2^2 = 4$ unique physical waveforms.
  • $k = 3$ (e.g., 000, 001, ...)
    A symbol is 3 bits. We need $2^3 = 8$ unique physical waveforms.
$$ M = 2^k $$

Where $M$ is the total number of distinct signal waveforms required, and $k$ is the number of bits per symbol.

2. Speed & Rates: Symbol Rate vs Bit Rate

Because one waveform now carries multiple bits, we have to be extremely careful when talking about "speed." We must explicitly separate how fast the signals are changing versus how fast the actual data is arriving.

Symbol Rate ($R_s$)

The number of physical signal waveforms transmitted per second. (Also called the signaling rate or baud rate).

$$ R_s = \frac{1}{T} $$

$T$ = Duration of one symbol

Bit Rate ($R_b$)

The total number of actual binary digits (0s and 1s) transmitted per second.

$$ R_b = k R_s = \frac{k}{T} = \frac{1}{T_b} $$

$T_b$ = Duration of one bit

The Takeaway:

Because each symbol carries $k$ bits, the Bit Rate is always $k$ times faster than the Symbol Rate. The physical transmitter can run slower ($R_s$) while delivering a massive amount of data ($R_b$)!

3. Dimensionality

How do we actually design these $M$ distinct signal waveforms? We use vector geometry!

  • One-Dimensional Signals
    All $M$ signals lie strictly on a single axis.
    Example: M-Ary Pulse Amplitude Modulation (PAM), where we only change the height of the wave.
  • Multidimensional Signals
    The $M$ signals are plotted across multiple axes, allowing complex constellation shapes like circles or grids.
    Example: Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM) or Phase-Shift Keying (PSK).
4. M-Ary Pulse Amplitude Modulation

The Core Concept

  • Take a single basic pulse shape $p(t)$.
  • Only change its amplitude (height).
  • Result: A 1-dimensional system ($M$-Ary PAM).
$$ s_m(t) = A_m p(t) = s_m \psi(t) $$

Derivation: Normalizing the Pulse

01 Create Unit-Energy Basis
  • Geometric basis functions MUST have $1$ Joule of energy.
  • Our physical pulse $p(t)$ has energy $\mathcal{E}_p$.
  • Action: Divide $p(t)$ by $\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_p}$.
$$ \psi(t) = \frac{p(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_p}} $$
Critical Concept: Why did we stop using s(t) and $\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}$ ?
  • In Binary (BPSK): There is only 1 fundamental signal. Therefore, the physical signal $s(t)$ is identical to the underlying pulse shape $p(t)$. Because there is only one signal, it only has one energy: Pulse energy = Bit energy ($\mathcal{E}_p = \mathcal{E}_b$).
  • In M-Ary PAM: We no longer have a single "$s(t)$", we have $M$ different signals ($s_1(t), s_2(t), \dots$) with $M$ different energies!
  • The Problem: We cannot define a basis function using "$s(t)$" or divide by a single "signal energy" because there are multiple signals with varying energies!
  • The Solution: We drop down to the lowest common denominator. We MUST define $\psi(t)$ purely based on the fundamental, shared "blank canvas" pulse shape: $p(t)$ and its fixed energy $\mathcal{E}_p$.
02 Equate Forms
  • Physical view: Pulse scaled by amplitude $A_m$.
  • Geometric view: Basis function scaled by coordinate $s_m$.
  • Action: Set them exactly equal.
$$ A_m p(t) = s_m \psi(t) $$
03 Solve for $A_m$
  • Substitute $\psi(t)$ from Step 1 into Step 2.
  • The $p(t)$ terms perfectly cancel out.
  • Result: Direct relationship between voltage $A_m$ and coordinate $s_m$.
$$ A_m = \frac{s_m}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_p}} $$

1D Nature

Because every signal $s_m(t)$ uses the exact same base pulse shape $p(t)$, they all lie perfectly on a single geometric axis.

Variable Energy

  • Different amplitudes = different signal energies!
  • Proof: Energy is the integral of the squared signal over time $T$.
$$ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{E}_m &= \int_{0}^{T} s_m^2(t) \, dt \\ &= \int_{0}^{T} (A_m p(t))^2 \, dt \\ &= A_m^2 \underbrace{ \int_{0}^{T} p^2(t) \, dt }_{\text{Definition of } \mathcal{E}_p} \end{aligned} $$
$$ \mathcal{E}_m = A_m^2 \mathcal{E}_p $$
5. Optimizing PAM Amplitudes

The Problem: DC Offset

  • Positive voltages (like $1V, 2V, 3V, 4V$) push the average voltage above zero.
  • This creates a massive DC offset.
  • The Cost: Wastes huge amounts of transmitted power.
INEFFICIENT:
[1V, 2V, 3V, 4V]

The Solution: Symmetry

  • Force amplitudes to be perfectly symmetric around zero.
  • The Benefit: Completely eliminates the DC component.
$$ A_m = (2m - 1 - M)A $$

Example ($M=4$): $-3A, -A, A, 3A$

The Result: Minimized Energy

  • Sum the squared symmetric amplitudes: $\frac{1}{M}\sum A_m^2$.
  • The Result: The average transmitted energy $\mathcal{E}_{av}$ elegantly collapses into an optimized formula.
1. The Raw Sum
$$ \mathcal{E}_{av} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} A_m^2 \mathcal{E}_p = \frac{A^2 \mathcal{E}_p}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} (2m - 1 - M)^2 $$
2. Expand & Solve Series
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{Expand Inner Square: } & \sum_{m=1}^{M} [4m^2 - 4m(M+1) + (M+1)^2] \\ \text{Apply } \sum m^2 \text{ and } \sum m: & 4\left[\frac{M(M+1)(2M+1)}{6}\right] - 4(M+1)\left[\frac{M(M+1)}{2}\right] + M(M+1)^2 \\ \text{Simplify Terms: } & \frac{2M(M+1)(2M+1)}{3} - 2M(M+1)^2 + M(M+1)^2 \\ \text{Combine Trailing Terms: } & \frac{2M(M+1)(2M+1)}{3} - M(M+1)^2 \\ \text{Factor out } M(M+1): & M(M+1) \left[ \frac{2(2M+1)}{3} - 1(M+1) \right] \\ \text{Common Denominator: } & M(M+1) \left[ \frac{4M + 2 - 3M - 3}{3} \right] \\ \text{Simplify Numerator: } & M(M+1) \left[ \frac{M - 1}{3} \right] \\ \text{Final Summation Result: } & \frac{M(M^2 - 1)}{3} \end{aligned} $$
3. Final Collapse
$$ \mathcal{E}_{av} = \frac{A^2 \mathcal{E}_p (M^2-1)}{3} $$

From Waveform to Constellation Coordinate

  • How do we map the physical signal waveform $s_m(t)$ to its exact 1D geometric coordinate $s_m$?
  • Recall that the physical amplitude is $A_m = A(2m - 1 - M)$.
  • To convert amplitude to the geometric coordinate space, we scale by the square root of the pulse energy $\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_p}$.
  • We define the fundamental distance scale factor as $d = A\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_p}$.
$$ \begin{aligned} s_m &= A_m \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_p} \\ &= [A(2m - 1 - M)] \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_p} \\ &= \underbrace{ A\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_p} }_{d} (2m - 1 - M) \\ s_m &= (2m - 1 - M)d \quad \text{for } m = 1, 2, \dots, M \end{aligned} $$

This beautifully simple equation generates the exact symmetrical constellation points shown above ($-5d, -3d, -d, d, 3d, 5d$) when you plug in $m=1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ for an $M=6$ system.

Example 5: $M=4$ PAM Waveforms & Energy

1. The Amplitudes: For $M=4$, the symmetric amplitudes are: $A_m \in \{-3A, -A, A, 3A\}$.
2. The Energy Derivation: $\mathcal{E}_{av} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathcal{E}_m$
$\mathcal{E}_{av} = \frac{1}{4} \left[ (-3A)^2 + (-A)^2 + (A)^2 + (3A)^2 \right] T$
$\mathcal{E}_{av} = \frac{1}{4}(9+1+1+9)A^2T = \mathbf{5A^2T}$
3. The Master Formula for Distance ($d$):
(Use this formula for exam questions to link geometry to physical energy!)

In signal space, the distance squared between two adjacent points equals the physical energy of the difference between their waveforms:
$$ \text{Distance}^2 = \int_0^T [s_{m+1}(t) - s_m(t)]^2 \, dt $$ $$ (2d)^2 = \int_0^T [2A \cdot p(t)]^2 \, dt = 4A^2 \underbrace{ \int_0^T p^2(t) \, dt }_{\mathcal{E}_p} = 4A^2 \mathcal{E}_p $$
General Rule for PAM: $\mathbf{d^2 = A^2 \mathcal{E}_p}$

In this specific example, the baseband pulse $p(t)$ is a rectangle of height 1 and duration $T$, so its energy is $\mathcal{E}_p = T$.
Substituting $\mathcal{E}_p = T$ into our general rule gives exactly: $\mathbf{d^2 = A^2T}$.
Therefore, $\mathcal{E}_{av} = 5A^2T = \mathbf{5d^2}$.

6. M-Ary Amplitude-Shift Keying (ASK)

The Bandpass Shift

  • To transmit over a bandpass channel, we must shift the signal to a high carrier frequency $f_c$.
  • Action: Multiply the baseband PAM signal $s_m(t)$ by a sinusoidal carrier $\cos(2\pi f_c t)$.
  • Spectrum: This creates a DSB-SC AM signal, shifting the spectrum to $\pm f_c$.
  • Bandwidth: The signal now occupies a channel bandwidth of $2W$.
$$ u_m(t) = s_m(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) $$
$$ U_m(f) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ S_m(f - f_c) + S_m(f + f_c) \right] $$
FAQ: Are there two cosines multiplied together?

A very common point of confusion when reading the lecture slides is comparing $s_m(t)$ and $u_m(t)$.
If Slide 41 looks like it says $s_m(t) = A_m p(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)$, it is a dangerous typo in the slides or a misreading of the notation. Let's be perfectly clear:

  • Baseband (No Carrier): $s_m(t) = A_m p(t)$. This is just a physical rectangular pulse with an amplitude. There is absolutely no cosine here.
  • Bandpass (With Carrier): $u_m(t) = s_m(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)$. This takes the baseband pulse and multiplies it by a carrier.
Therefore, substituting $s_m(t)$ into $u_m(t)$ yields:
$$ u_m(t) = \underbrace{ [A_m p(t)] }_{\text{Baseband Signal}} \cdot \underbrace{ \cos(2\pi f_c t) }_{\text{Single Carrier}} $$
There is only one carrier wave. The carrier is only applied exactly once to shift the baseband signal up to the bandpass frequency!

The Energy Halving Effect

  • What happens to the energy when we modulate with a carrier?
  • Because of the $\cos^2$ integration, the high-frequency $\cos(4\pi f_c t)$ term averages out to exactly zero.
  • The Critical Result: The energy of the bandpass signal is exactly one-half the energy of the baseband signal!
$$ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{E}_m &= \int_{0}^{T} u_m^2(t) \, dt \\ &= \int_{0}^{T} [s_m(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)]^2 \, dt \\ &= \int_{0}^{T} s_m^2(t) \cos^2(2\pi f_c t) \, dt \\ &= \int_{0}^{T} s_m^2(t) \left[ \frac{1 + \cos(4\pi f_c t)}{2} \right] \, dt \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{T} s_m^2(t) \, dt + \frac{1}{2} \underbrace{ \int_{0}^{T} s_m^2(t) \cos(4\pi f_c t) \, dt }_{\text{Averages to 0 for } f_c \gg W} \\ \mathcal{E}_m &= \frac{1}{2} \mathcal{E}_{m, \text{baseband}} \end{aligned} $$

The Basis Function & Dimensionality

  • To find the geometric basis function $\psi_c(t)$, we normalize the carrier-modulated pulse to have unit energy.
  • Notice that all $M$ signals can STILL be expressed entirely by scaling this single, shared basis function.
  • Dimensionality: Despite the high-frequency carrier, M-Ary ASK remains a strictly 1-dimensional ($N=1$) system.
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{Bandpass Pulse: } & p_c(t) = p(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) \\ \text{Energy Integral: } & \mathcal{E}_{p_c} = \int_0^T p^2(t) \cos^2(2\pi f_c t) \, dt \\ \text{Apply Trig Identity: } & \mathcal{E}_{p_c} = \int_0^T p^2(t) \left[ \frac{1 + \cos(4\pi f_c t)}{2} \right] \, dt \\ \text{High-Freq averages to 0: } & \mathcal{E}_{p_c} = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T p^2(t) \, dt = \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}_p \\ \text{Normalize to Unit Energy: } & \psi_c(t) = \frac{p_c(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{p_c}}} \\ \text{Substitute and Simplify: } & \psi_c(t) = \frac{p(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}_p}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\mathcal{E}_p}} p(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) \end{aligned} $$

Example 6: Determining Bandpass Coordinates

The Problem: A carrier-modulated PAM signal is expressed as $u_m(t) = s_m \psi(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)$, where $s_m = (2m - 1 - M)d$ is the geometric coordinate of the baseband signal, and $\psi(t)$ is a unit energy baseband pulse.

Determine: The bandpass basis function $\psi_c(t)$ and the new geometric coordinate $s_{cm}$ for the bandpass signal.
Step 1: Identify the "Shape" of the Signal
The signal equation is $u_m(t) = s_m \cdot [ \psi(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) ]$.
The constant $s_m$ is just the amplitude scaling factor. The actual physical "shape" of the waveform is the part in the brackets: $\psi(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)$.
Step 2: Find the Energy of the Shape
A fundamental rule of communication systems is that a geometric basis function must contain an energy of exactly $1$ Joule. Let's calculate the energy of our shape:
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{Energy} &= \int_{0}^{T} [\psi(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)]^2 \, dt \\ &= \int_{0}^{T} \psi^2(t) \left[ \frac{1 + \cos(4\pi f_c t)}{2} \right] \, dt \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{T} \psi^2(t) \, dt \quad \text{(since the high frequency term averages to 0)} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}(1) = \frac{1}{2} \quad \text{(since $\psi(t)$ is given as a unit energy pulse)} \end{aligned} $$
The energy is $1/2$. Because it is not $1$, this shape is not a valid basis function yet.
Step 3: Fix the Shape to Create the Basis Function $\psi_c(t)$
To create a valid unit-energy basis function, we must systematically divide the raw shape $v(t) = \psi(t)\cos(2\pi f_c t)$ by the square root of its energy $\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_v}$.
Applying this exact mathematical normalization formula gives us our official basis function:
$$ \begin{aligned} \psi_c(t) &= \frac{\text{Shape } v(t)}{\sqrt{\text{Energy of } v(t)}} \\ &= \frac{\psi(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)}{\sqrt{1/2}} \\ \psi_c(t) &= \sqrt{2} \psi(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) \end{aligned} $$
Step 4: Find the New Coordinate $s_{cm}$
We must rewrite the original signal equation $u_m(t)$ using our newly discovered basis function $\psi_c(t)$.
The original equation is $u_m(t) = s_m \cdot \psi(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t)$.
We want it to look like $u_m(t) = s_{cm} \cdot [ \sqrt{2} \psi(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) ]$.
To force the $\sqrt{2}$ to appear without breaking the math, we multiply and divide the whole equation by $\sqrt{2}$:
$$ \begin{aligned} u_m(t) &= s_m \cdot \psi(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) \\ u_m(t) &= \frac{s_m}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \left[ \sqrt{2} \psi(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) \right] \\ u_m(t) &= \underbrace{ \left( \frac{s_m}{\sqrt{2}} \right) }_{\text{New Coordinate } s_{cm}} \cdot \underbrace{ \left[ \sqrt{2} \psi(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) \right] }_{\text{Basis Function } \psi_c(t)} \end{aligned} $$
It is now completely obvious that the new bandpass coordinate is $s_{cm} = \frac{s_m}{\sqrt{2}}$.
