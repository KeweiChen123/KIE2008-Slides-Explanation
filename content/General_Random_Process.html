1. Probability Mass Function (PMF)

The Probability Mass Function (PMF) describes the exact probability that a discrete random variable $X$ takes on a specific value $x$. It is only defined for discrete variables (like integers).

  • Formula: $P_X(x) = P(X = x)$
  • Axiom 1: All probabilities must be non-negative: $P_X(x) \ge 0$
  • Axiom 2: The sum of all probabilities must equal 1: $\sum P_X(x_i) = 1$

ELI12: Imagine you have a standard 6-sided die. The PMF is like a bar chart where every number from 1 to 6 gets a vertical bar of height 1/6. You absolutely cannot roll a 2.5, so the bar at 2.5 is exactly zero. The PMF tells you the mass of probability at specific, distinct islands.

C) The running total of all probabilities up to a point.

2. Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)

The Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) evaluates the probability that a random variable $X$ takes a value less than or equal to $x$. It works for both discrete and continuous variables.

  • Formula: $F_X(x) = P(X \le x)$
  • Limits: $F_X(-\infty) = 0$ and $F_X(\infty) = 1$
  • Monotonicity: It is non-decreasing. For $a \le b$, $F_X(a) \le F_X(b)$

ELI12: Imagine you are collecting rainwater in a bucket during a storm. The CDF is the total amount of water in the bucket at any given time. It starts empty (0), fills up to the top (1), and can only stay the same or go up. It never goes down because water doesn't un-rain!

Quiz: Which property is strictly true for any valid CDF?

A) It is always a flat constant line.
B) It is a monotonically non-decreasing function bounded between 0 and 1.
C) Its total area must exactly equal 1.

3. Probability Density Function (PDF)

The Probability Density Function (PDF) describes the relative likelihood for a continuous random variable to take on a given value. The absolute probability of a continuous variable taking an EXACT single value is strictly zero; probability is instead measured as the area under the PDF curve over an interval.

  • Formula: $f_X(x) = \frac{d}{dx} F_X(x)$
  • Probability: $P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f_X(x) dx$
  • Total Area: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x) dx = 1$

ELI12: Imagine a solid block of Swiss cheese. The PDF is like measuring the microscopic density of the cheese at one exact pinpoint. To find the actual weight (probability) of a slice, you have to measure the volume of that entire slice! A single dimensionless pinpoint has no weight, just like a single continuous point has zero absolute probability.

Quiz: Can the value of a continuous PDF $f_X(x)$ at a specific point ever be strictly greater than 1?

A) No, because probabilities cannot exceed 1.
B) Yes, because it represents density, not absolute probability.
C) No, because its integral must be zero.

4. Joint Distributions

Joint Distributions describe the probabilistic relationship and simultaneous behavior of two or more random variables (e.g., $X$ and $Y$). The Joint PDF $f_{X,Y}(x,y)$ forms a 3D surface where the volume underneath represents probability.

  • Formula: $P(a \le X \le b, c \le Y \le d) = \int_c^d \int_a^b f_{X,Y}(x,y) dx dy$
  • Marginal PDF: $f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x,y) dy$ (Integrating out Y gives X)
  • Independence: If completely independent, $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x) \cdot f_Y(y)$

ELI12: Imagine tracking the height and weight of people. Height is one curve, weight is another. A Joint Distribution is like a 3D topological map showing where they overlap—telling you the probability of a person being exactly 6 feet tall AND weighing 180 pounds simultaneously.

Quiz: If two random variables X and Y are completely statistically independent, their Joint PDF is mathematically equal to:

A) The sum of their individual PDFs.
B) The product of their individual marginal PDFs.
C) Exactly zero everywhere.

5. Mean & Standard Deviation

The Mean $\mu = E[X(t)]$ is the expected value or DC offset of a signal. The Standard Deviation $\sigma = \sqrt{E[(X-\mu)^2]}$ is the square root of the variance, representing the root-mean-square (RMS) amplitude of the AC noise component.

Imagine a shaky video recorded on a drone. The Mean is the exact altitude the drone is trying to hover at (e.g., 50 meters). Because of the wind, the drone bounces up and down. The Standard Deviation is simply the "average bounce thickness"—say, ±2 meters. Mean is the target, Standard Deviation is the wobble!

Quiz: If you increase the standard deviation $\sigma$ of the drone's altitude, what happens to the signal?

A) The "wobble" thickness increases (more AC noise).
B) The drone hovers at a completely different target altitude.
C) The signal frequency shifts higher.

6. Stationary vs Non-Stationary Processes

A random process $X(t)$ is Strictly Stationary if its joint probability distribution does not change when shifted in time. Practically, this implies that the mean $\mu_X(t)$ and variance $\sigma^2_X(t)$ are constant over time.

Imagine a river. If it's a Stationary process, the water flow might look random, but the average water level and the average size of the waves stay exactly the same whether you look today or next year. If a storm comes and the river floods, the average changes—that's Non-Stationary!

Quiz: If a signal's average voltage drifts slowly upwards over an hour, is it stationary?

A) Yes, because the drift is slow.
B) No, because its mean is changing over time.
C) Yes, because it never drops to zero.

7. Variance (AC Power)

The Variance $\sigma^2 = E[(X-\mu)^2] = E[X^2] - \mu^2$ describes the power of the AC (fluctuating) component of a signal. It measures how spread out the random signal is around its mean.

If the mean μ is the depth of the ocean, the Variance is just how tall the waves are. We subtract the ocean depth because we only care about the turbulent splashing!

Quiz: Why does Variance represent AC power but completely ignore DC power?

A) Because variance subtracts the mean before calculating the square.
B) Because DC signals have infinite variance.
C) Because it is divided by the standard deviation.

8. Wide-Sense Stationary (WSS)

A process is WSS if:
1) Its mean is constant: $E[X(t)] = \mu$
2) Its autocorrelation depends only on the time difference τ: $E[X(t)X(t+\tau)] = R_X(\tau)$

We don't need the entire probability distribution to be perfectly invariant, just the first and second moments.

WSS is a relaxed version of Stationary. We only care about two things:
1. The average doesn't change.
2. How a point is related to its neighbor (the pattern of the wiggles) depends only on how far apart they are, not when they happen.

Quiz: For a process to be Wide-Sense Stationary (WSS), its Autocorrelation function $R_X(t_1, t_2)$ must only depend on:

A) The time difference $\tau = t_1 - t_2$.
B) The absolute starting time $t_1$.
C) The sum of the times $t_1 + t_2$.

9. Cyclostationary Processes

A process is Cyclostationary if its statistical properties (like mean and variance) vary periodically with time. This often happens when a random signal modulates a periodic carrier wave (e.g., AM/FM radio).

Imagine traffic on a highway. It's random, but there's a strict pattern: heavy at 8 AM, quiet at 2 AM. The randomness breathes in a repeating cycle. That breathing randomness is Cyclostationary!

Quiz: If you observe a temperature signal that reliably spikes every summer and drops every winter, what kind of stationarity is this?

A) Wide-Sense Stationary (WSS).
B) Cyclostationary.
C) Strictly Ergodic.

10. Ergodic Process (Ergodicity)

A WSS process is Ergodic if its time averages equal its ensemble averages.
$\lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \int x(t) dt = E[X(t)]$

This is crucial because in reality, we usually only have one realization of a signal to measure, not an infinite parallel universe of signals.

Ergodicity means: "Observing one person for a lifetime gives you the same average stats as observing the whole world for one second." If a process gets stuck in a rut and never explores all possibilities, it is NOT ergodic.

Ensemble (Many parallel signals at $t_0$)

Ensemble Avg: 0

Time (One signal over time $t$)

Time Avg: 0

11. Autocorrelation

Autocorrelation $R_X(\tau) = E[X(t)X(t-\tau)]$ quantifies the internal memory of a random process. It measures the inner product of a signal with a time-shifted version of itself. A high $R_X(\tau)$ means the signal value at time t strongly dictates the value at time t+τ.

Imagine shouting into a giant canyon. Autocorrelation measures how perfectly the returning echo matches your original shout. If the echo is instant (shift τ=0), it matches your shout perfectly! But as the echo bounces around the rocks and gets delayed longer and longer, it gets distorted. Autocorrelation is simply the "Echo Quality Score" as the delay gets longer.

1. The Signals (Original & Shifted)

2. The Multiplier Area ( $X(t) \times X(t-\tau)$ )

3. Autocorrelation Output RX(τ)

Quiz: If a signal is purely random white noise, what does its Autocorrelation $R_X(\tau)$ look like?

A) A flat horizontal line.

C) A perfect sine wave.

12. Auto-covariance

Auto-covariance $C_X(\tau) = E[(X(t)-\mu)(X(t-\tau)-\mu)]$ evaluates the correlation of only the dynamic variations (AC component) of a signal. By subtracting the mean μ before shifting and multiplying, we remove the artificial baseline correlation caused by a non-zero DC offset.

Imagine you are shouting into the canyon again, but this time you are standing on top of a 10,000-foot tall mountain. The standard Autocorrelation gets confused by the extreme altitude and gives a massive score just because you are high up. Auto-covariance subtracts the mountain's height first. It measures only the echo of the shout itself, completely ignoring the altitude you shouted from!

1. The Signals (High DC Offset)

2. Zero-Mean Signals & Product Area

3. Auto-covariance Output C_X(τ)

Quiz: When does Auto-covariance equal Autocorrelation?

A) When the signal has zero mean (no DC offset).
B) When the signal is purely white noise.
C) When the signal is purely a DC constant.

13. Cross-Correlation

Cross-correlation $R_{XY}(\tau) = E[X(t)Y(t-\tau)]$ measures the mathematical similarity between two entirely different signals, X and Y, as a function of the time delay $\tau$ between them. In Radar and GPS, we transmit a known pulse (Signal Y) and record the noisy echo (Signal X). By mathematically sliding the known pulse across the noisy recording and calculating the 'overlap area' at every step, the Cross-correlation function will abruptly spike to a maximum exactly when the two signals perfectly align. The delay $\tau$ at this spike reveals the exact arrival time of the echo!

Think of Cross-correlation like a high-tech metal detector. You program the detector with the exact magnetic signature of a gold coin (your known signal template). Then, you sweep it across a chaotic, trash-filled beach (the noisy received signal). The detector stays quiet until it sweeps directly over the gold coin, resulting in a massive *BEEP*! That spike tells you exactly where the target is buried.

1. The Beach (Noisy Signal) & Metal Detector (Template)

2. The Multiplier Area (Template * Beach)

3. Detector Output RXY(τ)

Quiz: Why is Cross-Correlation incredibly useful in Radar and GPS?

14. Average Power

The average power of a signal $x(t)$ over an interval $T$ is $P = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{T} \int |x(t)|^2 dt$. For a random process, average power is $E[X^2(t)]$, which is $R_X(0)$.

In electrical engineering, "Power" is proportional to the voltage squared. If a signal oscillates between +5V and -5V, its average voltage is 0, but its power is definitely not 0! That's why we square the signal first (so negatives become positive) and then average it.

Quiz: How does the Autocorrelation function $R_X(\tau)$ relate to the Average Total Power of a process?

B) The power is the total integral of the Autocorrelation from negative to positive infinity.
C) The power is evaluated by taking the limit as the time shift approaches infinity.

15. Power Spectral Density (PSD)

The PSD, $S_X(f)$, is the Fourier Transform of the autocorrelation function (Wiener-Khinchin theorem). It tells us how the power of a signal is distributed across different frequencies.

If "Power" tells you how bright a white light is, PSD puts that light through a prism and tells you exactly how much red, green, and blue light makes up the beam. It splits the random signal into frequencies!

Frequency Domain $S_X(f)$

16. Cross-Spectral Density (CSD)

The Cross-Spectral Density $S_{XY}(f) = \mathcal{F}\{R_{XY}(\tau)\}$ is the Fourier Transform of the cross-correlation function. It reveals which frequencies are shared between two different signals, ignoring unshared noise.

If you listen to two completely different songs at the same time, Cross-Spectral Density acts like a magic equalizer that only lights up if both songs happen to have a bassline hitting the exact same note. It finds the hidden frequencies that they share!

Signal $X(t)$ & Signal $Y(t)$

Shared Frequency Spectrum $S_{XY}(f)$
