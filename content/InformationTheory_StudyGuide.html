1. The Foundation of Information Theory

Claude Shannon (1948)

Founded in the groundbreaking paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication", this field completely redefined how we quantify, store, and communicate data.

The 3 Fundamental Questions

Information theory allows us to study communication systems from a purely mathematical perspective by answering three core questions:

  • Transmission Limit: What is the absolute highest rate at which information can be reliably transmitted over a communication channel?
  • Compression Limit: What is the absolute lowest rate at which information can be compressed, while still being perfectly retrievable (with zero or small errors)?
  • Complexity: What is the mathematical and systemic complexity required to build such optimal systems?

Shannon divided the universe of communication limits into two distinct theorems.

1. Source-Coding Theorem

  • Establishes limits that apply to the Information Sources themselves (Data Compression).
  • Goal: Minimum compression rate for perfect recovery.

2. Noisy Channel Coding Theorem

  • Establishes limits for transmitting over noisy physical mediums.
  • Goal: Maximum transmission rate reliably over a noisy channel.

2. Modeling the Information Source

The Element of Unpredictability

  • An information source produces the raw data to be transmitted to the destination.
  • Crucial Rule: The output of an information source MUST be a time-varying, unpredictable function.
  • Why? Because if a signal is perfectly predictable, it contains zero new information, and there is no reason to transmit it!
  • Therefore, all information sources are mathematically modeled as Random Processes.

Source Characteristics

The properties of the random process depend heavily on the nature of the source. We define them using:

  • Bandwidth, Range of Amplitude Variations, Power Content
  • Probability Density Function (PDF) & Power Spectral Density (PSD)

Real-World Example: Speech

Human speech signals can be mathematically modeled as a random process that happens to have almost all of its power heavily concentrated in a specific frequency band:

300 Hz - 3400 Hz

3. Discrete Memoryless Source (DMS)

Sampling the Source

Because most physical information sources (like voice) are bandlimited, they can be sampled at the Nyquist rate and perfectly reconstructed. Thus, we model the source as a sequence of discrete samples \( \{X_i\}_{i=-\infty}^{\infty} \).

What does "Memoryless" mean?

  • The DMS is one of the simplest mathematical models for an information source.
  • Memoryless: It generates outputs \( X_i \) that are completely independent of all previous outputs. The past has absolutely zero influence on the future.
  • Identically Distributed: The probabilities of each output do not change over time.
  • We mathematically define this as i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed).

The Alphabet & Probabilities

A full description of a DMS requires two things:

  • The Alphabet (\( \mathcal{A} \)): The set of all possible outputs. For example, a binary source has an alphabet of \( \mathcal{A} = \{0, 1\} \).
  • The Probabilities: The probability mass function (PMF) denoted by \( p_i = P(X = a_i) \).

4. Measuring Information: The 3 Intuitive Properties

Before we can talk about Entropy, we must mathematically define how to measure the raw information of a single event. To do this, Shannon established three strict intuitive properties that any mathematical formula for information must satisfy (Slides 12-14):

1. The Surprise Factor

The least likely output reveals the most information.

  • If I tell you the sun rose in the east today (100% probable), you gained 0 information.
  • If I tell you it snowed in the Sahara (highly improbable), that contains massive information!
  • Math Rule: The measure of information must be a decreasing function of probability.

2. Continuity

A small change in probability should not drastically alter the info.

  • If the probability of rain goes from 50% to 51%, the amount of information that event carries shouldn't suddenly spike to infinity.
  • Math Rule: The information measure must be a strictly continuous function of the source output's probability.

3. Additive Independence

If you subdivide an event into two independent parts, the total info is the sum of both.

  • If events A and B are independent, revealing A tells you nothing about B.
  • Math Rule: If \( p_j = p_{j1} p_{j2} \), then the information function MUST satisfy:
    \( I(p_j) = I(p_{j1}) + I(p_{j2}) \)

The Self-Information Formula

What is the only mathematical function in the universe that converts a multiplication (\( p_1 \times p_2 \)) into an addition (\( I_1 + I_2 \)) while also being a continuous, decreasing function?

The Logarithm!

\( I(p_i) = -\log_2(p_i) \)

Note: We use base 2 so that the unit of information is exactly bits per sample (or bits per symbol). If we used base 'e', the unit would be 'nats'.

4.5 Entropy (\( H(X) \)): Expected Information

Entropy (\( H(X) \))

While Self-Information measures a single event, Entropy is the average (expected) information content of the entire source. We calculate it by taking the weighted sum of all self-information across every possible output in the alphabet.

\( H(X) = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} p_i \log_2(p_i) \quad \text{[Unit: bits/sample]} \)

Mathematical Quirk: \( 0 \log_2(0) = 0 \)

Binary Memoryless Source Equation:

For a simple binary source with probability \( p \) and \( 1-p \), the formula explicitly expands to:

\( H(X) = -p\log_2(p) - (1-p)\log_2(1-p) \)
  • Upper Bound: Entropy is MAXIMIZED when all source outputs are equiprobable (Uniform distribution). \( H(X) = \log_2 N \).
  • Lower Bound: Entropy is ZERO when the output is deterministic (100% predictable).
  • \( 0 \le H(X) \le \log_2 N \)

Binary Entropy Function

For a binary source, Entropy \( H(X) \) peaks exactly at \( p = 0.5 \) (Binary Symmetric Source). This proves that maximum uncertainty yields maximum information!

Example Calculation: Source Rate

  • Problem (Slide 16): A source with bandwidth 4000 Hz is sampled at the Nyquist rate. It acts as a DMS with an alphabet of 5 values, and probabilities: \( \{ \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{16}, \frac{1}{16} \} \). Determine the data rate in bits/second.

Step 1: Calculate Entropy (bits per sample)

\( H(X) = - \left[ \frac{1}{2}\log_2(\frac{1}{2}) + \frac{1}{4}\log_2(\frac{1}{4}) + \frac{1}{8}\log_2(\frac{1}{8}) + \frac{2}{16}\log_2(\frac{1}{16}) \right] \)

\( H(X) = \frac{1}{2}(1) + \frac{1}{4}(2) + \frac{1}{8}(3) + \frac{2}{16}(4) = \mathbf{\frac{15}{8} \text{ bits/sample}} \)

Step 2: Apply Nyquist Rate

Nyquist Rate = \( 2 \times 4000 \text{ Hz} = 8000 \frac{\text{samples}}{\text{sec}} \)

Step 3: Calculate Final Bit Rate

Rate = \( 8000 \frac{\text{samples}}{\text{sec}} \times \frac{15}{8} \frac{\text{bits}}{\text{sample}} = \mathbf{15,000 \frac{\text{bits}}{\text{sec}}} \)

5. Joint Entropy & Mutual Information

Joint & Conditional Entropy

When dealing with sources that HAVE memory, we use Joint and Conditional entropy.

  • Joint Entropy \( H(X,Y) \): The total information in the combined system.
    \( H(X,Y) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(x,y) \)
  • Conditional Entropy \( H(X|Y) \): The remaining uncertainty in \( X \) after \( Y \) is completely known.
    \( H(X|Y) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(x|y) \)
Chain Rule:
\( H(X,Y) = H(Y) + H(X|Y) \)

Generalized Chain Rule (for N variables):
\( H(X_1 \dots X_n) = H(X_1) + H(X_2|X_1) + \dots + H(X_n|X_1 \dots X_{n-1}) \)

If independent: \( H(X_1 \dots X_n) = \sum H(X_i) \)

Mutual Information \( I(X;Y) \)

Mutual information measures how much uncertainty about \( X \) is completely removed simply by revealing \( Y \). It is the information they share.

\( I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y) \)
or explicitly:
\( I(X;Y) = \sum_{x}\sum_{y} p(x,y) \log_2 \left[ \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} \right] \)
  • It is perfectly symmetric! \( I(X;Y) = I(Y;X) \).
  • Can also be written as: \( H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \).

ELI5 Step-by-Step Example: Joint & Conditional Entropy (Slide 19)

The Setup: Two binary random variables \( X \) and \( Y \) have the following joint probabilities:

\( P(X=0, Y=1) = \frac{1}{4} \)    |    \( P(X=1, Y=1) = \frac{1}{2} \)    |    \( P(X=0, Y=0) = \frac{1}{4} \)
Note: \( P(X=1, Y=0) = 0 \) because the sum of all probabilities must be 1.

Step 1: Calculate Joint Entropy \( H(X,Y) \)

Use the formula: \( -\sum p(x,y) \log_2 p(x,y) \)

\( H(X,Y) = -\frac{1}{4}\log_2(\frac{1}{4}) - \frac{1}{2}\log_2(\frac{1}{2}) - \frac{1}{4}\log_2(\frac{1}{4}) - 0\log_2(0) \)

\( H(X,Y) = \frac{1}{4}(2) + \frac{1}{2}(1) + \frac{1}{4}(2) = \mathbf{\frac{3}{2} \text{ bits}} = 1.5 \text{ bits} \)

Step 2: Find Marginal Probabilities for X and Y

Sum the joint probabilities to find the individual (marginal) chances of X and Y occurring.

  • \( P(X=1) = P(X=1, Y=1) + P(X=1, Y=0) = \frac{1}{2} + 0 = \frac{1}{2} \)
  • \( P(X=0) = P(X=0, Y=1) + P(X=0, Y=0) = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} \)
  • \( P(Y=1) = P(X=1, Y=1) + P(X=0, Y=1) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4} \)
  • \( P(Y=0) = \frac{1}{4} \)

Step 3: Calculate Individual Entropies \( H(X) \) and \( H(Y) \)

  • Since \( X \) is perfectly uniform (50/50), its entropy is maximized: \( H(X) = 1 \text{ bit} \).
  • For \( Y \), we calculate \( H_b(0.75) \): \( -\frac{3}{4}\log_2(\frac{3}{4}) - \frac{1}{4}\log_2(\frac{1}{4}) \approx \) \( 0.81 \text{ bits} \).

Step 4: Calculate Conditional Entropies

Using the Chain Rule: \( H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y|X) = H(Y) + H(X|Y) \)

  • \( H(Y|X) = H(X,Y) - H(X) = 1.5 - 1 = \mathbf{0.5 \text{ bits}} \)
  • \( H(X|Y) = H(X,Y) - H(Y) = 1.5 - 0.81 = \mathbf{0.69 \text{ bits}} \)

ELI5 Step-by-Step Example: Mutual Information (Slide 21)

The Setup: Let \( X \) and \( Y \) be binary random variables perfectly uniformly distributed over exactly 3 specific outcomes:

\( P(X=0, Y=0) = \frac{1}{3} \)    |    \( P(X=1, Y=0) = \frac{1}{3} \)    |    \( P(X=0, Y=1) = \frac{1}{3} \)

Step 1: Calculate Marginals and Individual Entropy

First, find the probability that \( X=0 \) or \( Y=0 \) across all joint events.

  • \( P(X=0) = P(X=0, Y=0) + P(X=0, Y=1) = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3} \)
  • \( P(Y=0) = P(X=0, Y=0) + P(X=1, Y=0) = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3} \)
  • Because both have a \( \frac{2}{3} \) chance of being 0, their individual entropies are identical:
    \( H(X) = H(Y) = H_b(\frac{2}{3}) \approx \mathbf{0.919 \text{ bits}} \).

Step 2: Calculate Joint Entropy \( H(X,Y) \)

Since the joint probability is uniformly distributed over exactly 3 values, its entropy hits the absolute upper bound formula \( \log_2(N) \):

\( H(X,Y) = \log_2(3) \approx \mathbf{1.585 \text{ bits}} \)

Step 3: Calculate Conditional Entropy \( H(X|Y) \)

Using the Chain Rule:

\( H(X|Y) = H(X,Y) - H(Y) = 1.585 - 0.919 = \mathbf{0.666 \text{ bits}} \)

Step 4: Solve for Mutual Information \( I(X;Y) \)

Finally, calculate the Mutual Information (the information they share)!

\( I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y) = 0.919 - 0.666 = \mathbf{0.253 \text{ bits}} \)

6. Continuous Sources (Differential Entropy)

Discrete-Time Continuous-Alphabet

  • If a source outputs real continuous numbers (like pure analog voltage), standard entropy breaks down.
  • Why? To flawlessly reconstruct an analog value with infinite decimal precision, you would need an infinite number of bits!
  • Instead, we use Differential Entropy \( h(X) \), integrating over the Probability Density Function \( f_X(x) \):
\( h(X) = - \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x) \log f_X(x) \,dx \)

Uniform Distribution \( [0, a] \):

\( h(X) = \log_2(a) \)

Zero-Mean Gaussian (Variance \( \sigma^2 \)):

\( h(X) = \frac{1}{2} \ln(2\pi e \sigma^2) \)

Derived using facts: \( \int f_X(x) = 1 \) and \( \int x^2 f_X(x) = \sigma^2 \)

Note for Continuous Mutual Information: The formulas remain structurally identical to the discrete version, just swapping \( H \) for \( h \):
\( I(X;Y) = h(Y) - h(Y|X) = h(X) - h(X|Y) \)

7. The Source Coding Theorem (Data Compression)

The Essence of Data Compression

  • According to Shannon's Source Coding Theorem, an information source can be encoded with an arbitrarily small error probability at any rate \( R \) bits/output, as long as \( R > H(X) \).
  • If \( R < H(X) \), error probability is bounded away from zero regardless of system complexity.
  • Important Note (Slide 31): Shannon's theorem only gives the mathematical limit for the existence of source codes. It does NOT provide the actual algorithm to do the encoding!
  • This works because of "Typical Sequences" (sequences that match the expected statistical probabilities of the source). As \( n \) grows large, atypical sequences approach zero probability.

Deriving the Mathematical Limit

Using the memoryless property, the probability of a typical sequence is:

\( P(X=x) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} p_i^{np_i} \)

If we apply \( \log_2 \) to both sides to solve for \( x \), it beautifully simplifies to:

\( P(X=x) = 2^{-nH(X)} \)

Because the total probability is almost 1, the total number of Typical Sequences is the reciprocal: \( 2^{n H(X)} \).
To digitally encode them, we only need exactly \( nH(X) \) bits!

  • If the source is purely Uniform (maximum entropy), then \( 2^{n \log_2 N} = N^n \). You need the full bit-width. Zero compression is possible.
  • However, if the source has skewed probabilities, \( H(X) \) shrinks, allowing for massive data compression!

What about Sources WITH Memory? (Slide 30)

If a source has memory, previous outputs reveal information about future ones. The rate of "fresh" information decreases as more outputs are revealed!

For these stationary sources, the Entropy Rate is defined as the limit as \( n \to \infty \):

\( H = \lim_{n \to \infty} H(X_n | X_1, X_2, \dots X_{n-1}) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} H(X_1, X_2, \dots X_n) \)

8. Huffman Source Coding Algorithm

Fixed-to-Variable Length Coding

The Source Coding Theorem proved compression is possible, but didn't provide the algorithm to do it. Huffman coding is the practical algorithm that actually achieves this compression!

  • The Core Idea: Map more frequently occurring sequences to shorter binary code words, and less frequent ones to longer code words.
  • The Prefix Condition: To ensure unique decodability without spacing/synchronization errors, no code word can be the prefix (start) of another code word. It must be instantaneously decodable.
  • Among all prefix-condition codes, Huffman achieves the absolute minimum average code word length.

Average Length (\( \bar{R} \))

\( \bar{R} = E[L] = \sum p(x) l(x) \)

It satisfies the boundary condition:
\( H(X) \le \bar{R} < H(X) + 1 \)

Efficiency (\( \eta \))

\( \eta = \frac{H(X)}{\bar{R}} \)

The closer \( \bar{R} \) is to \( H(X) \), the closer the efficiency is to 100%.

The 5-Step Huffman Algorithm (Slide 36)

How do we actually build the tree?

  1. Sort source outputs in decreasing order of their probabilities.
  2. Merge the two least probable outputs into a single output whose probability is the sum of the two.
  3. If the number of remaining outputs is 2, go to Step 4; otherwise loop back to Step 2.
  4. Arbitrarily assign '0' and '1' as code words for the two remaining merged outputs.
  5. Trace back: If an output is a merger, append the current code word with a '0' and a '1' to obtain the specific code words for the preceding branches. Stop when all original outputs have codes.

Major Drawbacks of Huffman Coding

  • Requires Source Statistics: You must know the exact probabilities beforehand. If you don't, you must make two passes over the data (one to calculate stats, one to compress).
  • Ignores Source Memory: If designed for block length 1, it only optimizes for frequency. To optimize for memory, you must increase block length, which exponentially explodes the complexity of the algorithm.

Prefix Condition & Synchronization (Slide 33-34)

Let's look at 4 different ways to encode an alphabet of 5 letters \( (a_1 \dots a_5) \) with probabilities \( (\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{16}, \frac{1}{16}) \):

Letter Prob Code 1 Code 2 Code 3 (Huffman) Code 4
\(a_1\)\( \frac{1}{2} \)11000
\(a_2\)\( \frac{1}{4} \)01101001
\(a_3\)\( \frac{1}{8} \)00110011010
\(a_4\)\( \frac{1}{16} \)00011000111011
\(a_5\)\( \frac{1}{16} \)00001100001111110
Average Length \(\bar{R}\) 1.93751.93751.8752.0625

Code Analysis

  • Codes 1 & 2: Both are self-synchronizing. However, Code 2 needs to wait for the first bit of the next code to confirm the current code ended. Code 1 is instantaneous.
  • Code 3 (Huffman): Satisfies the Prefix Condition (no code is the prefix of another), is instantaneously decodable, AND hits the absolute minimum average length (1.875).
  • Code 4 Problem: It is NOT uniquely decodable! For example, the sequence 110 could be decoded as \( a_5 \) (110) OR as \( a_4, a_1 \) (11, 0). This causes total failure at the receiver.

ELI5 Breakdown: Improving Huffman with Extensions (Slide 39)

The Setup: We have a memoryless source with 3 perfectly equiprobable letters \( A = \{a_1, a_2, a_3\} \). Thus, \( p = \frac{1}{3} \) for all.
The basic Huffman code assigns them: 0, 10, 11.

Step 1: The Baseline (Block Length 1)

  • Entropy: \( H(X) = \log_2(3) \approx \mathbf{1.585 \text{ bits}} \).
  • Average Code Length: \( \bar{R} = \frac{1}{3}(1 \text{ bit}) + \frac{1}{3}(2 \text{ bits}) + \frac{1}{3}(2 \text{ bits}) = \mathbf{1.667 \text{ bits}} \).

Step 2: The Extended Source (Block Length 2)

What if we group them in pairs? \( A^2 = \{a_1 a_1, a_1 a_2, \dots , a_3 a_3\} \). Since there are 3 letters, there are \( 3 \times 3 = 9 \) possible pairs. Each pair now has a probability of \( \frac{1}{9} \).

If we run the Huffman algorithm on these 9 pairs, we generate 9 new codes: 7 of them have a length of 3 bits, and 2 of them have a length of 4 bits.

Step 3: Calculating Improved Efficiency

Let's calculate the new Average Code Length (\( R_2 \)) for the pair:

\( R_2 = \frac{1}{9}(7 \times 3) + \frac{1}{9}(2 \times 4) = \frac{21}{9} + \frac{8}{9} = \mathbf{3.22 \text{ bits}} \)

But wait! That 3.22 bits represents TWO letters. We must divide by 2 to find the average per single letter:

\( \frac{3.22}{2} = \mathbf{1.61 \text{ bits per source output}} \)

Conclusion: By encoding blocks of 2 instead of 1, we dropped the required bits per letter from 1.667 down to 1.61, moving us much closer to the absolute Entropy limit of 1.585!

9. Lempel-Ziv Source Coding Algorithm

Universal Source Coding

To overcome Huffman's flaws, Lempel-Ziv was created. It is a universal algorithm, meaning it operates entirely independently of the source statistics!

  • Variable-to-Fixed Length: It parses variable-length phrases of data and encodes them into fixed-length binary words.
  • The Dictionary Approach: It parses data by identifying the smallest phrase length that has not appeared before, adding it to a dictionary.
  • The encoded format is mathematically represented as:
    [Dictionary Location of Prefix] + [New Source Output Bit].

Example: Parsing the Dictionary (Slide 42 & 45)

Watch how LZ breaks apart a binary string by looking for the shortest new sequence it hasn't seen yet:

Raw String: 0100001100001010000010100...

Parsed: 0, 1, 00, 001, 10, 000, 101, 0000, 01, 010...

Notice how every chunk is formed by taking a previous chunk and appending exactly 1 new bit to it!

Index Phrase LZ Codeword Construction
1 (0001)A(0, A) → Since 'A' is new, prefix is index 0.
2 (0010)B(0, B) → Since 'B' is new, prefix is index 0.
3 (0011)AB(1, B) → 'A' is at Index 1, append 'B'.
4 (0100)ABC(3, C) → 'AB' is at Index 3, append 'C'.

The Efficiency Reality Check: At the very beginning of a file, Lempel-Ziv might actually increase file size (e.g., mapping 44 bits to 75 bits). However, as the length of the sequence grows massive, the dictionary becomes incredibly efficient, asymptotically approaching \( nH(X) \).

The Overflow Problem (Slide 44): What happens when the dictionary gets too large? In reality, any fixed number of phrases will eventually overflow memory. To solve this, the encoder/decoder must actively purge dictionaries of old/useless elements to make room for new ones. This exact memory-purging implementation is what powers modern .zip and .gzip files!

10. Modeling Communication Channels

The Channel System

A communication channel is any physical medium (coaxial cable, fiber optics, free space) that transmits information. Because bandwidth is limited, we use the Nyquist sampling theorem to model practical analog waveform channels as Discrete-Time Channels.

Physical Impairments (Slide 47)

Why is the output different from the input? It gets crushed by a stochastic mix of attenuation, nonlinearities, bandwidth limits, multipath propagation, and thermal noise.

  • If it has no memory, it is a Discrete Memoryless Channel (DMC).
  • Described by input alphabet \( \mathcal{X} \), output alphabet \( \mathcal{Y} \), and conditional transition probabilities \( P(y|x) \).
  • For a DMC: \( P(y|x) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(y_i|x_i) \).

Binary Symmetric Channel (BSC)

The simplest digital channel model. It transmits binary bits, but introduces a crossover probability \( \epsilon \) (the chance a 0 flips to a 1, or 1 flips to 0 due to noise).

\( \epsilon = P(0|1) = P(1|0) \)

AWGN Connection (Slide 51-52)

If the BSC is actually representing an Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) channel using Binary PSK antipodal signaling, the crossover error is explicitly defined by the Q-function:

\( \epsilon = Q\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{N_0}}\right) \)

Where the analog noise PDF is: \( f(y|x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} e^{\frac{-(y-x)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \)

Power Constraint: To make the math realistic, the channel imposes a fixed transmission power constraint over \( n \) samples: \( \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2 \le P \)

11. Channel Capacity & The Noisy Channel Theorem

The Ultimate Speed Limit

The Noisy Channel Coding Theorem proves a mind-blowing fact: Noise does not limit the reliability of a channel; it only limits its SPEED.

You can achieve reliable communication (arbitrarily small error probability) over ANY noisy channel, provided your transmission rate \( R \) is less than the Channel Capacity \( C \).

\( C = \max_{P(x)} I(X;Y) \)

ELI5 Analogy: "Far Apart" Images (Slide 54-55)

Imagine transmitting letters over a noisy channel. If you send an "a", the noise might blur it so it looks like a "d" at the receiver. The receiver has ambiguity.

The Solution: The transmitter and receiver agree beforehand to ONLY use the letters "a" and "c". By selecting inputs that are "far apart," their noisy outputs (images) will have negligible overlap, completely eliminating ambiguity! This is the essence of the Noisy Channel theorem.

Deriving Capacity for the BSC (Slide 57 & 59)

How do we mathematically prove the maximum rate for a noisy BSC? We look at the Extension Channel (block length \( n \)).

  • Out of \( n \) transmitted bits, the channel flips roughly \( n\epsilon \) bits.
  • The number of possible error combinations is given by the binomial coefficient, which approximates to: \( 2^{n H_b(\epsilon)} \).
  • The total number of highly probable output sequences is \( 2^{n H(Y)} \).

To ensure the receiver doesn't get confused (ambiguity), the input sequences must be mapped to outputs that do NOT overlap. The maximum number of non-overlapping sequences \( M \) is:

\( M = \frac{2^{n H(Y)}}{2^{n H_b(\epsilon)}} = 2^{n(H(Y) - H_b(\epsilon))} \)

Since Rate \( R = \frac{\log_2 M}{n} \), the rate becomes \( H(Y) - H_b(\epsilon) \). To maximize this rate, we set the input \( X \) to perfectly uniform (50/50), which forces \( H(Y) = 1 \). Leaving us with the final formula:

\( C_{BSC} = 1 - H_b(\epsilon) \)

Example: The 12-Transmission Cost (Slide 62)

Given a channel where the conditional entropy \( H(Y|X) \) is calculated as exactly \( 1.5 \) bits, and the max output entropy \( H(Y)_{max} = \log_2(3) \approx 1.585 \) bits.

  • Step 1: Calculate Capacity (Mutual Info).
    \( C = I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X) \)
  • \( C = 1.585 - 1.5 = \mathbf{0.085 \frac{\text{bits}}{\text{transmission}}} \)
  • Step 2: Time Cost. If capacity is only 0.085 bits per transmission, how many transmissions to send 1 full bit?
  • \( \frac{1 \text{ bit}}{0.085 \frac{\text{bits}}{\text{transmission}}} \approx \mathbf{12 \text{ transmissions!}} \)

Example: AWGN Capacity (Slide 63)

Find the capacity of a telephone channel with Bandwidth \( W = 3000 \) Hz, and SNR = 39 dB.

  • Step 1: Convert dB to Linear.
    \( 39 \text{ dB} \rightarrow 10^{3.9} \approx 7943 \).
  • Step 2: Apply Shannon's Formula.
    \( C = 3000 \log_2(1 + 7943) \)
  • Result: \( C = 3000 \log_2(7944) \approx \mathbf{38,000 \frac{\text{bits}}{\text{sec}}} \)

Example: Reliable Transmission (Slide 70)

Transmit source U with \( P(0) = \frac{1}{4} \) reliably over a BSC. Find max crossover error \( \epsilon \).

  • Step 1: Source Entropy.
    \( H(U) = H_b(0.25) \approx 0.8113 \text{ bits} \).
  • Step 2: Apply Bound. Reliable transmission requires \( H(U) < C \).
  • \( 0.8113 < 1 - H_b(\epsilon) \implies H_b(\epsilon) < 0.1887 \).
  • Result: Numerically solving yields \( \mathbf{0 \le \epsilon < 0.0288} \).

12. The Bounds of Communication (Trade-offs)

Bandwidth vs. Power

The channel capacity formula \( C = W \log_2\left(1 + \frac{P}{N_0 W}\right) \) reveals a critical engineering trade-off. We can compensate for a lack of bandwidth by increasing power, and vice versa!

  • Increasing Power (\( P \)): Allows for more distinct, noise-immune amplitude levels. However, the increase in capacity is strictly logarithmic, meaning it is a very slow, expensive way to increase speed.
  • Increasing Bandwidth (\( W \)): Allows more samples per second, increasing rate. However, more bandwidth admits more thermal noise (\( N_0 W \)), deteriorating the signal.

The Absolute Infinity Limit (Slide 65)

Because of the thermal noise penalty, letting bandwidth \( W \to \infty \) does NOT result in infinite capacity. It mathematically asymptotes to a hard limit:

\( \lim_{W \to \infty} W \log_2\left(1 + \frac{P}{N_0 W}\right) \approx 1.44 \frac{P}{N_0} \)

This proves that by increasing bandwidth alone, you cannot increase capacity to infinity.

Spectral Efficiency (\( r \)) vs Energy Efficiency (\( \mathcal{E}_b/N_0 \)) (Slide 69)

By dividing rate by bandwidth (\( r = R/W \)), we can define the required Energy per bit to Noise ratio to maintain reliable communication:

\( \frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{N_0} > \frac{2^r - 1}{r} \)
  • The Absolute Minimum: As \( r \to 0 \), the absolute lowest power ratio required to transmit data without errors is \( 0.693 \) (or -1.6 dB). Below this, communication is mathematically impossible.
  • Power-Limited Regime (\( r \ll 1 \)): Bandwidth is large, but power is heavily restricted (e.g., deep space probes). The primary concern is saving power, so we use high-dimensional schemes like Orthogonal or Simplex signaling.
  • Bandwidth-Limited Regime (\( r \gg 1 \)): Power is plentiful, but frequency spectrum is heavily restricted (e.g., mobile cellular networks). The primary concern is saving spectrum, so we use crowded, low-dimensional constellations like M-ary QAM and PSK!

Revision 6(b): Comprehensive Channel Analysis

Problem: Let \( X \) be a binary random variable with \( P(X=0) = p \). Let \( Y \) depend on \( X \) through the crossover probability \( P(Y=1|X=0) = P(Y=0|X=1) = e \). Find all entropies, and analyze maximization.

Part A: Finding All Entropies

  • 1. Source Entropy \( H(X) \): Because \( P(X=0) = p \), it is exactly the binary entropy function.
    \( H(X) = H_b(p) = -p\log_2(p) - (1-p)\log_2(1-p) \)
  • 2. Marginal Output Entropy \( H(Y) \): Using Total Probability, the chance of receiving a 0 is: \( P(Y=0) = P(Y=0|X=0)P(X=0) + P(Y=0|X=1)P(X=1) \).
    So \( P(Y=0) = p(1-e) + (1-p)e \). Thus:
    \( H(Y) = H_b\big(p(1-e) + e(1-p)\big) \)
  • 3. Conditional Entropy \( H(Y|X) \): Because the crossover is symmetric (\( e \)), the uncertainty introduced by the channel is entirely independent of the input!
    \( H(Y|X) = H_b(e) \)
  • 4. Joint Entropy \( H(X,Y) \): Use the Chain Rule \( H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y|X) \):
    \( H(X,Y) = H_b(p) + H_b(e) \)
  • 5. Reverse Conditional \( H(X|Y) \): Use the Chain Rule in reverse \( H(X|Y) = H(X,Y) - H(Y) \):
    \( H(X|Y) = H_b(p) + H_b(e) - H_b\big(p(1-e) + e(1-p)\big) \)
  • 6. Mutual Information \( I(X;Y) \): The shared information \( I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X) \):
    \( I(X;Y) = H_b\big(p(1-e) + e(1-p)\big) - H_b(e) \)

Part B: For a fixed \( e \), which \( p \) maximizes \( I(X;Y) \)?

We want to maximize capacity. Since \( H(Y|X) \) only depends on the fixed \( e \), we must maximize \( H(Y) \).
A binary entropy function peaks when its input is exactly \( 0.5 \) (Uniform distribution).
So we set \( P(Y=0) = 0.5 \):
\( p(1-e) + e - pe = 0.5 \)
\( p(1-2e) = 0.5 - e \)
\( p = \frac{0.5 - e}{1-2e} = \frac{0.5(1-2e)}{1-2e} = \mathbf{0.5} \)
Conclusion: A perfectly uniform input (\( p = 0.5 \)) maximizes the mutual information!

Part C: For a fixed \( p \), which \( e \) minimizes \( I(X;Y) \)?

The absolute minimum possible value for Mutual Information is 0 (meaning no information gets through). This happens when the input and output are completely independent.
If we set the crossover error \( e = 0.5 \), the receiver basically flips a coin regardless of what was transmitted. Let's verify:
If \( e = 0.5 \), \( H(Y|X) = H_b(0.5) = 1 \). And \( H(Y) = H_b(0.5) = 1 \).
\( I(X;Y) = 1 - 1 = \mathbf{0} \).
Conclusion: \( e = 0.5 \) (a 50% error rate) mathematically destroys the channel, minimizing the mutual information to exactly zero.

Revision 6(b) Q2: Capacity of the 3x3 Channel

Problem: Find the capacity of the discrete memoryless channel with input \( X=\{a,b,c\} \), output \( Y=\{a,b,c\} \), and the following transition probability matrix \( P(Y|X) \):

\( \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.3 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.3 & 0.2 \\ 0.2 & 0.5 & 0.3 \end{bmatrix} \)

Step 1: Calculate Conditional Entropy \( H(Y|X) \)

Notice that every single row in the matrix is just a permutation of the exact same probabilities: {0.2, 0.3, 0.5}. Because entropy does not care about order, the conditional entropy for EVERY input is identical!

\( H(Y|X=a) = H(Y|X=b) = H(Y|X=c) = -0.2\log_2(0.2) - 0.3\log_2(0.3) - 0.5\log_2(0.5) \)

\( H(Y|X) \approx 0.464 + 0.521 + 0.500 = \mathbf{1.485 \text{ bits}} \)

Step 2: Maximize Output Entropy \( H(Y) \)

To find the channel capacity, we must maximize the mutual information \( I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X) \). Since \( H(Y|X) \) is fixed at 1.485, we just need to maximize \( H(Y) \).

The output \( Y \) has 3 possible states {a,b,c}. The maximum possible entropy for a 3-state variable occurs when it is perfectly uniform (\( P(a) = P(b) = P(c) = \frac{1}{3} \)):

\( H(Y)_{max} = \log_2(3) \approx \mathbf{1.585 \text{ bits}} \)

Note: Because the columns of the transition matrix sum to 0.9, 1.1, and 1.0 (not symmetric), achieving exactly uniform output requires slightly non-uniform inputs, but the theoretical maximum output entropy remains \( \log_2(3) \).

Step 3: Calculate Channel Capacity \( C \)

Subtract the fixed noise penalty from the maximum output entropy:

\( C = H(Y)_{max} - H(Y|X) = 1.585 - 1.485 = \mathbf{0.1 \text{ bits/transmission}} \)

14. 1-Hour Exam Cram: The Ultimate Cheat Sheet

1. The "Big 6" Entropy Formulas

If you get a joint probability matrix \( P(X, Y) \), here is the exact step-by-step to find everything:

  • Step 1: Find Marginals \( P(X) \) and \( P(Y) \)
    Sum the rows of the matrix to get \( P(X) \). Sum the columns to get \( P(Y) \).
  • Step 2: Base Entropies \( H(X) \) and \( H(Y) \)
    Apply the standard formula using the marginals you just found:
    \( H(X) = - \sum P(x) \log_2 P(x) \)
  • Step 3: Joint Entropy \( H(X,Y) \)
    Apply the standard formula to every single non-zero cell inside the joint matrix:
    \( H(X,Y) = - \sum_{x,y} P(x,y) \log_2 P(x,y) \)
  • Step 4: Conditional Entropies \( H(Y|X) \) and \( H(X|Y) \)
    Never calculate these manually! Use the Chain Rule:
    \( H(Y|X) = H(X,Y) - H(X) \)
    \( H(X|Y) = H(X,Y) - H(Y) \)
  • Step 5: Mutual Information \( I(X;Y) \)
    The amount of info shared between sender and receiver. Use the Venn Diagram rule:
    \( I(X;Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \)
    Alternatively: \( I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X) = H(X) - H(X|Y) \)

2. Channel Capacity Algorithm

Capacity \( C \) is the maximum possible mutual information. To find it:

  • Step 1: Calculate Conditional Entropy \( H(Y|X) \)
    Look at the Transition Matrix \( P(Y|X) \). If every row is just a permutation of the same probabilities, \( H(Y|X) \) is a constant. Calculate it for just one row.
  • Step 2: Maximize Output Entropy \( H(Y) \)
    Entropy is maximized when the output is perfectly uniform (equiprobable). If \( Y \) has \( N \) possible states, then:
    \( H(Y)_{max} = \log_2(N) \)
  • Step 3: Subtract to find C
    \( C = H(Y)_{max} - H(Y|X) \text{ [bits/transmission]} \)

AWGN (Continuous) Capacity: If given Bandwidth (\( W \)) and Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), use Shannon's formula. Make sure to convert SNR from dB to linear first (\( 10^{\text{SNR}_{dB}/10} \))!

\( C = W \log_2(1 + SNR_{linear}) \text{ [bits/sec]} \)

3. Source Coding & Transmission Rates

  • Average Code Length (\( \bar{R} \))
    Multiply the length of each code word by its probability, and sum them up:
    \( \bar{R} = \sum p_i \cdot \text{length}(code_i) \text{ [bits/sample]} \)
  • Nyquist Sampling Rate (\( f_s \))
    If given a bandwidth \( W \), the Nyquist rate is always double the bandwidth.
    \( f_s = 2W \text{ [samples/sec]} \)
  • Total Source Bit Rate
    Multiply the bits per sample by the samples per second:
    \( \text{Rate} = \bar{R} \times f_s \text{ [bits/sec]} \)

4. Math Survival Kit

  • Log Base Conversion: If your calculator only has \( \ln \) or \( \log_{10} \):
    \( \log_2(x) = \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(2)} \)
  • The Zero Rule: In entropy calculations, if a probability is 0:
    \( 0 \log_2(0) = 0 \)
  • Binary Entropy Function \( H_b(p) \):
    \( H_b(p) = -p\log_2(p) - (1-p)\log_2(1-p) \)
  • Maximums:
    Binary entropy peaks at 1.0 when \( p=0.5 \).

5. Continuous (Differential) Entropy

If dealing with continuous waveforms instead of discrete states:

  • Uniform Distribution \([0, a]\)
    \( h(X) = \log_2(a) \)
  • Zero-Mean Gaussian (Variance \( \sigma^2 \))
    \( h(X) = \frac{1}{2} \log_2(2\pi e \sigma^2) \text{ bits} \) (Use \(\ln\) instead of \(\log_2\) if the answer asks for "nats")

6. The Shannon Theoretical Limits

Exam questions love to ask for theoretical boundaries as parameters go to infinity:

  • Absolute Minimum Energy (\( E_b/N_0 \))
    For reliable communication to exist, \( E_b/N_0 \) can never drop below the Shannon limit:
    \( \frac{E_b}{N_0} \ge \ln(2) \approx 0.693 \implies \mathbf{-1.6 \text{ dB}} \)
  • Infinite Bandwidth Capacity
    As Bandwidth \( W \to \infty \), capacity does NOT go to infinity! It hits a hard ceiling:
    \( C_{\infty} \approx 1.44 \frac{P}{N_0} \text{ [bits/sec]} \)

7. High-Yield Minutiae

  • Self-Information of a Single Message:
    If asked for the information of just one specific event (not average entropy):
    \( I(x_i) = -\log_2 P(x_i) \text{ bits} \)
  • Extended Huffman Efficiency Bounds:
    If we encode blocks of length \( n \) letters at a time, the efficiency bound tightens to:
    \( H(X) \le \bar{R} < H(X) + \frac{1}{n} \)
  • Spectral Bit Rate vs. Energy:
    Spectral Bit Rate \( r = \frac{R}{W} \text{ (bits/s/Hz)} \)
    Energy per bit \( E_b = \frac{P}{R} \)
