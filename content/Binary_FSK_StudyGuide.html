Binary Frequency-Shift Keying (BFSK)

Orthogonal signaling, frequency separation conditions, and geometric representation

Chapter 1: The Physical Intuition

What is Binary FSK?

The Core Concept

Instead of changing the amplitude to send data (like in BASK), we change the frequency of the carrier wave.

The Signals

Both signals are transmitted with the exact same power (amplitude). The only difference is how fast they oscillate.

How we assign bits in BFSK:

  • Bit 1 ➔ Transmit at frequency $f_1$: $s_1(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}} \cos(2\pi f_1 t)$
  • Bit 0 ➔ Transmit at frequency $f_2$: $s_2(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b}} \cos(2\pi f_2 t)$

Chapter 2: The Orthogonality Condition ($f_b$, $k_1$, $k_2$)

What did your lecturer mean by the $f_b$ condition?

In BFSK, we want $s_1(t)$ and $s_2(t)$ to be orthogonal. Physically, this means they do not interfere with each other at the receiver. Mathematically, orthogonality requires their inner product (the integral of their multiplication over one bit period $T_b$) to equal zero.

Step 1 The Inner Product

We set up the inner product of the two signals and demand it equals zero:

$$ \int_{0}^{T_b} s_1(t) s_2(t) dt = \frac{2\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b} \int_{0}^{T_b} \cos(2\pi f_1 t) \cos(2\pi f_2 t) dt = 0 $$

Step 2 The Product-to-Sum Identity

To solve the integral, we use the trigonometric identity: $\cos(A)\cos(B) = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(A+B) + \cos(A-B)]$.

$$ \frac{\mathcal{E}_b}{T_b} \int_{0}^{T_b} \left[ \cos(2\pi (f_1+f_2)t) + \cos(2\pi (f_1-f_2)t) \right] dt = 0 $$

Step 3 The High-Frequency Trick (Again!)

The sum frequency $(f_1+f_2)$ is extremely high, meaning its integral evaluates to zero (just like in ASK). We are left strictly with the difference frequency term:

$$ \int_{0}^{T_b} \cos(2\pi (f_1-f_2)t) dt = 0 $$

Step 4 Integrating to solve for frequency separation

If we integrate the cosine, we get a sine function. For the sine of a value to equal zero, the angle must be a multiple of $\pi$. Let's call this multiple $m$ (where $m$ is an integer: $1, 2, 3...$).

$$ \left[ \frac{\sin(2\pi (f_1-f_2)t)}{2\pi(f_1-f_2)} \right]_0^{T_b} = 0 \quad \implies \quad 2\pi(f_1 - f_2)T_b = m\pi $$

We divide out $\pi$ to find the required frequency separation $\Delta f = |f_1 - f_2|$:

$$ 2 \Delta f T_b = m \quad \implies \quad \Delta f = \frac{m}{2T_b} $$
The Finale Linking it to $f_b$ and $k_1, k_2$

Your lecturer brought up $f_b$ (the bit rate) and the $k$ integers. Here is exactly how they define the FSK signal:

1. The Bit Rate Connection Because the bit rate $f_b = \frac{1}{T_b}$, we can substitute it directly into our separation formula:
$$ \Delta f = m \frac{f_b}{2} $$
2. Minimum Separation For orthogonality to exist, $m$ must be an integer ($\ge 1$). Thus, the absolute minimum frequency distance is:
$$ \Delta f_{\text{min}} = \frac{f_b}{2} $$
3. The $k$ Integers If we lock our transmit frequencies to $f_1 = \frac{k_1}{2T_b}$ and $f_2 = \frac{k_2}{2T_b}$, the separation mathematically forces $m = |k_1 - k_2|$:
$$ \Delta f = \frac{|k_1 - k_2|}{2T_b} $$
The Lecturer's Conclusion:
To guarantee that BFSK signals don't interfere with each other (perfect orthogonality), the two frequencies must be separated by an integer multiple of half the bit rate ($\frac{f_b}{2}$). We easily achieve this physical requirement by ensuring $k_1$ and $k_2$ are chosen as distinct integers!

Chapter 3: Basis Functions and Geometric Representation

Building the Signal Space

Unlike BASK which is 1-Dimensional (all points lie on a single line), BFSK uses two orthogonal signals. This means we require two separate dimensions (an X-axis and a Y-axis) to represent the signals geometrically!

The Two Basis Functions:

$$ \psi_1(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{T_b}} \cos(2\pi f_1 t) \quad \text{and} \quad \psi_2(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{T_b}} \cos(2\pi f_2 t) $$

By using these unit-energy basis functions, we can represent our two bit signals as simple vectors:

  • $s_1(t) = \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b} \cdot \psi_1(t)$ ➔ Geometrically, this is the coordinate vector $(\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b}, 0)$
  • $s_2(t) = \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b} \cdot \psi_2(t)$ ➔ Geometrically, this is the coordinate vector $(0, \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_b})$

Chapter 4: PPM vs FSK & The Bandwidth Tradeoff

Why does PPM require so much more bandwidth?

FSK Pulse Width ($T_s$)

In FSK, the sine wave runs continuously for the entire duration of the symbol time $T_s$. The "pulse width" is the full $T_s$.

PPM Pulse Width ($T_p$)

In PPM, the symbol time $T_s$ is sliced into $M$ discrete time slots. The pulse only exists in one specific slot, so its width is drastically smaller: $T_p = \frac{T_s}{M}$.

Visualizing ⚡: The Fourier Tradeoff

In signal processing, Time and Frequency are inversely related. A signal that is very wide in the time domain is very narrow in the frequency domain, and vice versa.
Bandwidth $BW \approx \frac{1}{\text{Pulse Width}}$

$$ BW \approx \frac{1}{T_s} $$

The Bandwidth Conclusion:
  • The PPM Spike: Because a PPM pulse must squeeze all of its energy into a tiny time slot $T_p$, it violently turns on and off.
  • Frequency Splatter: This sharp, sudden change in the time domain causes its energy to splatter widely across the frequency domain.
  • The $M$ Factor: As $M$ increases, $T_p$ gets even narrower, meaning the required bandwidth for PPM explodes linearly with $M$.
  • The FSK Advantage: FSK, using smooth, continuous waves that last the full $T_s$, avoids this splatter and requires far less bandwidth.

Key Terms Glossary

Orthogonal Signaling Two signals that have an inner product of zero over the bit period. They do not interfere with one another at the receiver.
Frequency Separation ($\Delta f$) The physical distance in Hertz between the frequency $f_1$ (Bit 1) and frequency $f_2$ (Bit 0).
Bit Rate ($f_b$ or $R_b$) The speed at which bits are transmitted, equal to $\frac{1}{T_b}$. It directly dictates the minimum $\Delta f$ required for orthogonality.
Pulse Width ($T_p$) The actual duration of time that physical signal energy is being transmitted within a symbol period $T_s$.
Bandwidth ($BW$) The range of frequencies a signal occupies. Due to Fourier properties, $BW \approx \frac{1}{T_p}$.
