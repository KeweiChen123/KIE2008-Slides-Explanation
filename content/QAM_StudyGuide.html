Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM)

Beyond the Circle: Combining Digital Amplitude and Phase Modulation for Maximum Data Density.

1. Breaking the Circle: The Core Concept

Removing the Energy Restriction

  • The PSK Limitation: In PSK, all symbols have perfectly identical energy (\( \mathcal{E}_s \)), forcing them onto a tight 1D circle in the vector plane.
  • The QAM Solution: QAM completely removes this constant energy restriction.
  • The Result: Symbols can now be scattered anywhere across the entire 2D Cartesian plane, allowing us to pack vastly more data without crowding the points together as severely!

Combined Modulation

  • Two-in-One: QAM is fundamentally a combination of both Amplitude Modulation (PAM) and Phase Modulation (PSK).
  • Quadrature Carriers: We impress separate information bits onto two orthogonal carriers simultaneously:
cos(2πfct)   and   -sin(2πfct)

2. Mathematical Formulation & Derivations

The General QAM Waveform

  • The transmitted QAM signal dynamically modulates the amplitude of both the In-Phase and Quadrature carriers:
\( u_m(t) = A_{mc} g_T(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) - A_{ms} g_T(t) \sin(2\pi f_c t) \)
  • \( \{A_{mc}\} \) and \( \{A_{ms}\} \): Sets of discrete amplitude levels generated by mapping \( k \)-bit sequences.

Deriving Cartesian from Polar

QAM can also be viewed in polar form (simultaneous amplitude and phase modulation):

\( u_{mn}(t) = A_m g_T(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t + \theta_n) \)

Derivation: Using the trig identity \( \cos(A+B) = \cos A \cos B - \sin A \sin B \):

  • Expand: \( A_m \cos(2\pi f_c t + \theta_n) \)
    \( = A_m \left[ \cos(\theta_n)\cos(2\pi f_c t) - \sin(\theta_n)\sin(2\pi f_c t) \right] \)
  • By substituting:
    \( A_{mc} = A_m \cos(\theta_n) \)
    \( A_{ms} = A_m \sin(\theta_n) \)
  • We return precisely to our Cartesian Equation!

Symbol Rate & Bit Rate

If we combine two PAM signals, where the In-Phase carrier uses \( M_1 \) levels and Quadrature uses \( M_2 \) levels:

  • Let \( M_1 = 2^{k_1} \) and \( M_2 = 2^{k_2} \).
  • The total number of unique symbols is \( M = M_1 \times M_2 \).
  • The total bits transmitted per symbol is \( k = k_1 + k_2 = \log_2(M_1 M_2) \).
  • Symbol Rate Equation: The speed at which symbols are sent (\( R_s \)) relates to the bit rate (\( R_b \)) by:

\( R_s = \frac{R_b}{k_1 + k_2} \)

3. Geometric Representation (16-QAM)

The 16-QAM Rectangular Grid

Unlike PSK, which forms a single ring, QAM forms a densely packed 2D grid. 16-QAM is created by combining two independent 4-PAM signals on orthogonal axes.

Vector Coordinates

\( \mathbf{s}_m = \left[ \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} A_{mc}, \ \sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s} A_{ms} \right] \)

Orthonormal Basis

\( \psi_1(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s}} g_T(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) \)

\( \psi_2(t) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_s}} g_T(t) \sin(2\pi f_c t) \)

Euclidean Distance

\( d_{mn} = \|\mathbf{s}_m - \mathbf{s}_n\| \)
(Calculated via Pythagorean theorem)

4. Average Energy & Error Probability Derivations

Average Symbol Energy (\( \mathcal{E}_{av} \))

  • Because points are scattered at different distances from the origin, symbols do not have the same energy!
  • The corners of the 16-QAM grid require much more power to transmit than the inner four points.
  • The Derivation: Assuming all \( M \) symbols are equally likely to be transmitted, the average transmitted energy is exactly the statistical expected value (the sum of the squared distances from the origin to each point, divided by \( M \)):
\( \mathcal{E}_{av} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \|\mathbf{s}_m\|^2 \)

Probability of Error Bounds

  • Because QAM is essentially two independent PAM signals running in parallel, the total probability of correct reception is the product of the probabilities of correct reception for each PAM signal.
  • This allows us to firmly bound the Error Probability (\( P_M \)) for a rectangular QAM constellation:
\( P_M \le 1 - \left[1 - 2Q \left( \sqrt{ \frac{3\mathcal{E}_{av}}{(M-1)N_0} } \right) \right]^2 \)

Which simplifies to our primary upper bound formula (where \( k = \log_2 M \) and \( \mathcal{E}_{b,av}/N_0 \) is the SNR per bit):

\( \mathbf{P_M \le 4Q \left( \sqrt{ \frac{3k \mathcal{E}_{b,av}}{(M-1)N_0} } \right)} \)

5. The Ultimate Showdown: M-ary QAM vs M-ary PSK

Why QAM Dominates for Large Data

  • If we want to transmit an immense amount of data, we need a large \( M \). But which modulation scheme is better?
  • We can compare their performance mathematically by taking the ratio of the arguments inside their respective \( Q \)-functions.
  • Derivation Intuition: The argument inside the \( Q \)-function represents the effective Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR). A higher argument inside the \( Q \)-function means a lower error probability.

The SNR Advantage Ratio (\( \Re_M \))

\( \Re_M = \frac{(P_M)_{QAM}}{(P_M)_{PSK}} = \frac{3 / (M-1)}{2 \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi}{M}\right)} \)

Let's plug in the numbers to see what happens as \( M \) grows:

Constellation Size (\( M \)) Ratio \( \Re_M \) (QAM Advantage)
M = 4 (4-QAM vs QPSK) Ratio is exactly 1.0 (They are mathematically identical)
M = 8 (8-QAM vs 8-PSK) Ratio is \( \approx \) 1.46 (QAM pulls ahead)
M = 16 (16-QAM vs 16-PSK) Ratio is \( \approx \) 2.63 (QAM absolutely dominates)

Conclusion: A large ratio indicates a massive SNR advantage for QAM. Because QAM utilizes the entire 2D plane rather than just the perimeter of a circle, it keeps symbols spaced much further apart, drastically reducing the error rate for high-speed internet!

6. Example Walkthrough: Average Power of 8-QAM

Exam Question: Constellation Efficiency

  • Problem: Consider an \( M=8 \) rectangular signal constellation (Constellation A) and an \( M=8 \) combined PAM-PSK circular constellation (Constellation B).
  • Assuming that adjacent signal points in each constellation are separated by a minimum distance of \( d = 2 \)...
  • Question: Determine the average transmitted power for each constellation, and determine which is more power efficient.

Step-by-Step Solution

Part 1: Constellation A (Rectangular Grid)

  • In a standard 8-point rectangular grid where points are separated by \( d=2 \), the points sit at coordinates like \( (\pm 1, \pm 1) \) and \( (\pm 3, \pm 1) \).
  • 4 inner points: Have a distance to the origin of \( \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2} \).
  • 4 outer points: Have a distance to the origin of \( \sqrt{3^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{10} \).
  • Average Power (\( P_A \)):
    \( P_A = \frac{1}{8} \left[ 4 \times (\sqrt{2})^2 + 4 \times (\sqrt{10})^2 \right] = \frac{1}{8} [ 4(2) + 4(10) ] = \frac{48}{8} = 6 \)
  • In Decibels: \( 10 \log_{10}(6) \approx \mathbf{7.8 \ dB} \)

Part 2: Constellation B (Combined PAM-PSK)

  • In this circular layout, points sit on two rings. For \( d=2 \):
  • 4 inner ring points: Have a distance to the origin of \( 2 \).
  • 4 outer ring points: Have a distance to the origin of \( 1 + \sqrt{3} \).
  • Average Power (\( P_B \)):
    \( P_B = \frac{1}{8} \left[ 4 \times (2)^2 + 4 \times (1 + \sqrt{3})^2 \right] \approx 4.7 \)
  • In Decibels: \( 10 \log_{10}(4.7) \approx \mathbf{6.7 \ dB} \)

Final Answer: Constellation B is more power efficient. Because its average power is \( 6.7 \text{ dB} \) compared to Constellation A's \( 7.8 \text{ dB} \), Constellation B requires about 1.1 dB less power to transmit the exact same signal with the exact same error protection (\( d=2 \)).
